Abstract
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization provides a gradient-free alternative to first-order
(FO) methods by estimating gradients via finite differences of function evaluations,
and has recently emerged as a memory-efficient paradigm for fine-tuning large-
scale models by avoiding backpropagation. However, ZO optimization has a
fundamental tension between accuracy and query efficiency. In this work, we show
that this can be substantially improved by unifying two complementary principles:
(i) a projection-based subspace view that reduces gradient estimation variance by
exploiting the intrinsic low-rank structure of model updates, and (ii) Muon-style
spectral optimization that applies gradient orthogonalization to extract informative
spectral structure from noisy ZO gradients. These form a unified framework
of subspace gradient orthogonalization, which we instantiate in a new method,
ZO-Muon, admitting a natural interpretation as a low-rank Muon optimizer in
the ZO setting. Extensive experiments on large language models (LLMs) and
vision transformers (ViTs) demonstrate that ZO-Muon significantly accelerates
convergence and achieves a win–win improvement in accuracy and query/runtime
efficiency. Notably, compared to the popular MeZO baseline, ZO-Muon requires
only 24.7% of the queries to reach the same SST-2 performance for LLM fine-
tuning, and improves accuracy by 25.1% on ViT-L fine-tuning on CIFAR-100.
Code is available at https://github.com/OPTML-Group/ZO-Muon.
1 Introduction
Training deep models such as LLMs [1–3] relies on backpropagation (BP) to compute first-order
(FO) gradients for optimization [4, 5]. However, as model sizes scale, the GPU memory cost of
BP grows substantially [6]. This motivates a fundamental question with practical impact: Can deep
models be trained in a gradient-free manner without relying on BP? Toward this end, zeroth-order
(ZO) optimization [7–9] offers a theoretically grounded alternative to FO optimization by estimating
gradients via finite differences of function values using only forward passes of the model, thereby
substantially reducing the memory footprint during training [10–13]. Although ZO optimization
also arises in black-box or gray-box application settings [14–17] (where FO gradients are infeasible
or inaccessible), memory-efficient fine-tuning of large models has recently become one of its most
impactful and arguably primary applications, as first proposed in [10] and a focus of this work.
Despite the promise of ZO optimization, achieving convergence and accuracy comparable to FO
optimization remains highly nontrivial. A key challenge of ZO optimization is the high variance of
query-based gradient estimation, which becomes increasingly severe as the problem dimension grows
[8, 9, 17, 20, 21]. Therefore, to reduce the variance of ZO gradient estimation, existing work has
focused on designing improved gradient estimators. Examples include preconditioning the estimator
with Hessian information [19, 22], optimizing perturbation directions before computing directional
derivatives [23], and imposing structural priors such as sparsity [13, 24] or low-rankness [18, 25] in
the gradient estimation. However, these designs still primarily operate in the original parameter space
of the model, which limits the extent of variance reduction. As a result, a significant performance gap
still remains between ZO and FO methods in deep model training.

Another principled approach to reducing variance in ZO optimization is to use multiple queries (at the
cost of increased query complexity) to average out estimation noise. However, a linear increase in the
number of queries (or runtime) yields only a sub-linear improvement in performance (and may even
lead to a multi-query paradox with no improvement) [26], making it challenging to strike a favorable
accuracy-efficiency trade-off. For example, FZOO [27] requires a substantial number of queries to
estimate the variance of function evaluations for gradient normalization. Similarly, SVRG-based
ZO methods [21, 28] also require an excessive number of queries to construct error-correction terms
for determining descent directions from estimated gradients. ZO-Align [26] leverages projection
alignment across multiple queries, but is difficult to scale because it must store multiple ZO gradients
in large matrices. MUZO [29] takes not only multiple queries but also the more complex Adam
optimizer in ZO optimization, which incurs additional memory overhead.
As mentioned above, although ZO optimization for model training has made substantial recent
progress, its core bottlenecks, limited accuracy due to high variance and a poor accuracy-efficiency
trade-off, remain unresolved. Thus, we ask:
(Q) Can we advance ZO training to achieve a win-win in both accuracy and efficiency?
To address (Q), we propose a novel subspace gradient orthogonalization viewpoint to revisit and
redesign ZO optimization. This framework consists of two key components: projection-based
subspace ZO optimization and gradient-orthogonalized spectral optimization. The former reduces
the variance of ZO gradient estimation by projecting the original full-space problem into a lower-
dimensional subspace. The latter further improves optimization accuracy by leveraging gradient
orthogonalization in the recent Muon optimizer [30, 31], which can extract meaningful descent
directions from noisy ZO gradient estimates rather than directly using their entry-wise values. We
term the proposed subspace gradient orthogonalization-based ZO optimization approach “ZO-Muon”
to echo its connection to Muon. As shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2, ZO-Muon consistently outperforms
a range of state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines in both convergence speed and accuracy. Moreover,
these performance gains do not incur additional query cost under the same runtime budget, indicating
improved efficiency in both runtime and query usage across LLM and ViT fine-tuning. This achieves
the desired win-win outcome posed in (Q). We summarize our main contributions as follows.
① (Formulation) We revisit ZO optimization from a projection-based perspective and propose a
subspace randomized gradient estimator (Subspace RGE), which substantially reduces the variance
of gradient estimation by operating in a low-dimensional subspace.
② (Algorithm) We show that Muon’s gradient orthogonalization provides complementary gains when
meeting Subspace RGE, yielding an enhanced ZO optimization algorithm, termed ZO-Muon.
③ (Practice) Compared with the stateful baselines including MeZO [10], LOZO [18], SparseMeZO
[13], HiZOO [19], SubZero [25] and FZOO [27], ZO-Muon consistently delivers superior accuracy
and efficiency; see Figs. 1–2 for representative results.

2 Related Work
This work connects three lines of research: (i) ZO optimization and its applications to deep models,
(ii) subspace optimization for memory- and compute-efficient training, and (iii) emerging gradient-
orthogonalized optimizers for large-scale model training. See detailed review in Appendix A. Among
existing studies, the most closely related is JAGUAR-Muon [32], which attempts to directly apply
gradient orthogonalization (GO) to full-space ZO momentum, but incurs additional memory and
runtime overhead over MeZO [10], and shows very limited empirical gains. This is likely because
applying GO to noisy, high-dimensional ZO estimates amplifies non-informative spectral components,
a limitation also confirmed by our work. By contrast, we show that GO becomes effective for ZO
only when coupled with a subspace formulation, which we term subspace gradient orthogonalization.
3 Rethinking ZO Optimization: A Projected Subspace View
In this section, we first introduce the standard ZO optimization formulation, whose core algorithmic
component is a query-based ZO gradient estimator built from finite differences of function values.
We then develop a new projection-based subspace perspective, from which we derive a subspace ZO
gradient estimator and motivate its effectiveness for improving ZO accuracy and query efficiency.

objective, i.e., EΨ[f (X + µΨ)], and its estimation variance scales as O(mn/Nq) [7]. Consequently,
a fundamental limitation of RGE is its high gradient estimation variance, which scales proportionally
with the problem dimension and can become prohibitively large for training large-scale models. In
theory, mitigating this variance requires using a large number of queries Nq, leading to an unfavorable
variance-query trade-off.
Subspace ZO optimization: A projection-based view. A growing body of evidence suggests that,
in large model training (especially fine-tuning), the weights and their gradients evolve within a much
lower-dimensional subspace [33–36]. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether one can transition ZO
optimization from the full space to a subspace since the reduced effective dimension can significantly
lower the variance of RGE.

Comparison with prior work: A closer look at Subspace RGE. The last equality in (6) suggests
that Subspace RGE can be implemented in the same form as the standard RGE (2), but using PΨ
as the effective perturbation which admits a low-rank factorization into P ∈ Rm×r and Ψ ∈ Rr×n
since r ≪ min{m, n}. Here, we omit the query index of Ψ for brevity. This perspective connects
the proposed Subspace RGE to the existing low-rank ZO optimization approach, LOZO [18]. We
provide a detailed comparison between our proposal and LOZO below.
LOZO [18] incorporates low-rank perturbations into the (central-difference) RGE in (2), yielding the
low-rank gradient estimator (LGE).
ˆ∇Xf (X) = f (X + µAB) − f (X − µAB) 2µ AB, (7)
where A ∈ Rm×r and B ∈ Rr×n are two random matrices from standard Gaussian distributions
with r ≪ min{m, n}. The overall perturbation AB thus gives a low-rank, factorized approximation
of the full-space perturbation Ψ in (2).
A comparison between the LGE in (7) and the proposed Subspace RGE in (6) shows that the two
estimators take essentially the same form, up to a swap between the left perturbation matrices A and
P, and between the right perturbation matrices B and Ψ. However, beyond this formal similarity,
there exists a key difference in the choice of the (left) perturbation matrix: Subspace RGE enforces P
to be column-orthonormal, whereas LGE imposes no such constraint on A. This distinction is critical
because P in Subspace RGE admits a natural interpretation as a subspace projector, which is precisely
why it estimates the projected gradient ˆP∇Xf (X) in step ② of (4), where ˆP = PP⊤. Recall that ˆP
is a projection matrix satisfying the idempotence property, and it is easy to verify that (I − ˆP) is also
a projection matrix onto the subspace orthogonal to the column space of P. The above indicates that
the full gradient naturally admits the decomposition ∇Xf (X) = ˆP∇Xf (X) + (I − ˆP)∇Xf (X),
where (I − ˆP) is the complementary projection. Therefore, the use of a column-orthonormal matrix
P in Subspace RGE endows the ZO method with a clear geometric interpretation about the FO
gradient component it aims to estimate. By contrast, LOZO does not admit such an interpretation.

4 ZO-Muon: Boosting Subspace ZO via Gradient Orthogonalization
In this section, we show that gradient orthogonalization (GO), the core ingredient underlying the
recently popular optimizer Muon [30, 31], can be used to further boost the subspace ZO method.
Gradient orthogonalization in Muon: From FO to ZO, with insights from the failure of ZO-
Muon-V0. Before introducing ZO-Muon, we briefly recall the key idea behind FO Muon. Muon can
be interpreted as a steepest-descent method under a spectral-norm constraint [39], or more broadly as
a spectral optimization method that exploits the matrix-level spectral structure of descent directions
rather than their entry-wise information. Concretely, given the iterate Xt of the optimization variable
X at iteration t and the learning rate ηt, the basic Muon (without momentum) takes the form [30]:
Xt+1 = Xt − ηt msign(Gt), (8)
5

where Gt denotes the (stochastic) gradient at iteration t, and msign(·) denotes the matrix sign
function, also known as matrix whitening transformation. The quantity msign(Gt) can be interpreted
as the descent direction obtained by projecting Gt onto the spectral-norm unit ball [40], which admits
the closed-form expression:
msign(G) = G(G⊤G)− 1 2 = U sign(Σ) V⊤ = U[:,:k] V⊤ [:,:k], (9)
where G = UΣV⊤ is the singular value decomposition (SVD) of G, and sign(·) denotes the (entry-
wise) sign function, returning 1 for non-zero (positive) singular values and 0 otherwise. Moreover,
U[:,:k] ∈ Rm×k and V[:,:k] ∈ Rn×k denote the submatrices of U and V consisting of their first k
columns, respectively, where k is the rank of G.
As shown in (9), the msign operation plays the role of GO by equalizing all active singular directions
and producing a spectrally isotropic update. This leads to the key benefit in Muon, which enables the
model update to fully explore and exploit the spectral descent directions. In addition, Muon provides
an efficient numerical approximation of msign(·) via Newton–Schulz (NS) iterations [39], avoiding
the need to compute a full SVD.
Inspired by Muon’s spectral advantage, one may naturally ask whether exploiting such spectral
information in the ZO gradient estimator can also bring similar benefits as in FO optimization.
Accordingly, by replacing Gt with its standard RGE counterpart in (2), we obtain a vanilla ZO-Muon
variant, which we denote as ZO-Muon-V0, i.e., applying GO directly to MeZO.

validated by ZO-Muon in Fig. 5, this synergy yields a clear accuracy–efficiency win–win.

Projection matrix sampling strategy: Why random is used. Based on Prop. 1, the projection
matrix ideally comes from the SVD of FO gradients. In ZO settings, however, this is infeasible.
Motivated by the empirical success of using a random column-orthonormal projection matrix P
in Subspace RGE (6), we adopt a practical strategy of lazily sampling P as a random column-
orthonormal projection. While one might think that an informed projection matrix P in (10), such
as one derived from the moving average of past ZO gradients, would better align with the true
gradient’s principal components, our empirical results suggest otherwise. As detailed in Table A3 of
Appendix I, Gaussian sketching [42] to past descending directions yields no notable performance
gains over random sampling with either Subspace-MeZO or ZO-Muon. Furthermore, maintaining the
historical momentum matrix increases GPU memory consumption, which counteracts the primary
memory-efficiency motivation of ZO fine-tuning.
Hyperparameters in ZO-Muon: Query number Nq and projection rank r. Two hyperparameters
play a central role in ZO-Muon: the number of queries Nq in (10) and the projection rank r of P.
First, we find that setting the query number Nq > 1 is crucial for ZO-Muon, e.g., Nq = 4 works well
in our experiments, indicating that the RGE in (10) should be implemented in its multi-query form.
Recall that in the absence of msign, the method reduces to Subspace RGE, yielding Subspace-MeZO
as examined in Fig. 3. For both Subspace-MeZO and its full-space baseline MeZO [10], the default
choice is Nq = 1. This is due to the multi-query paradox [26]: Although using multiple queries
per iteration can reduce the variance of the gradient estimate, whether it improves optimization
performance depends on how the queries are aggregated. In particular, simple averaging with Nq > 1
can lead to diminishing returns (i.e., the multi-query paradox) compared to using a single query.

For the other key hyperparameter, the projection rank r that defines the subspace via P, we find that

SVD vs. Newton–Schulz in ZO-Muon. Thanks to the subspace projection in ZO-Muon (10), the

exact SVD as in (9), instead of using NS iterations [30]. Thus, Fig. 8 compares the runtime and

forward passes for ZO gradient estimation, and the msign computation. As shown, using SVD for
exact msign computation indeed improves accuracy over the NS-based implementation. However,

efficiency gap is the primary reason we adopt NS in ZO-Muon.

6 Conclusion
In this paper, we propose ZO-Muon, a novel ZO optimization method that bridges the gap between
memory efficiency and convergence speed in deep model training. By combining subspace optimiza-
tion with spectral gradient orthogonalization, ZO-Muon resolves the fundamental variance–efficiency
tension in standard ZO methods. Extensive experiments on LLMs and ViTs show that ZO-Muon
consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in both performance and efficiency. We refer
readers to Appendix J-L for discussions on limitations, ethics statement, and LLM usage.

Appendix
A Detailed Related Work
ZO optimization and applications in deep models. ZO optimization [7–9, 49–51] estimates
gradients via finite differences of function values without computing FO derivatives. As detailed in
Sec. 1 and thus omitted here for brevity, a large body of foundational work on ZO optimization has
focused on reducing estimation variance to improve accuracy and the trade-off with query efficiency.
Empirically, the effectiveness of ZO methods has been demonstrated in solving small-scale problems,
such as input-level black-box adversarial attack and defense [14, 15, 52–56], input-level prompt
design for foundation models [57–59], and resource-limited on-device learning [60–62]. More
recently, ZO optimization has expanded to large-scale deep model training, including black-box or
non-differentiable settings [17, 63] and memory-efficient fine-tuning [10–13]. Focusing on the latter,
to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to demonstrate a win–win improvement in both
accuracy and efficiency via subspace gradient orthogonalization.
Subspace optimization for efficient training. Since the weights or gradients in deep model training
often exhibit low-rank structure [33–36, 64], a growing body of work has explored training in
low-dimensional subspaces to improve memory efficiency [65–68]. Notably, LoRA-style methods
[34, 69–72] directly constrain trainable parameters to low-rank matrices, but can be less effective in
some settings due to the limited expressiveness of low-rank parameterizations [64] and insufficient
directional diversity [71]. Subspace perspectives have been explored in ZO optimization. Prior
work [37, 38] develops theoretical subspace formulations but does not address practical large-scale
fine-tuning, while more practical methods like LOZO [18] and SubZero [25] use low-dimensional
Gaussian matrices for perturbation and updates. As we show later, our method clearly outperforms
these approaches by exploiting the variance-reduction benefits of subspace ZO optimization in the
spectral domain.
Emerging gradient-orthogonalized methods for deep model training. Recent years have witnessed
growing interest in gradient-orthogonalized optimization methods for training large-scale deep
models, particularly foundation models, driven by the need to exploit the matrix-structured and
spectral properties of gradients that are poorly captured by conventional vector-based optimizers. The
Muon optimizer [30] and its subsequent variants [31, 40, 41, 73–77] embody this shift by performing
gradient orthogonalization (GO), which projects gradient updates onto a spectrally constrained
set via matrix whitening and can be interpreted as a steepest-descent step under a spectral-norm
constraint [39]. This spectral treatment leads to faster convergence and reduced optimizer-state
memory compared to Adam, enabling Muon to scale to foundation-model training, most notably
in Moonlight [31], a 3B/16B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model. Despite this progress, GO has
remained largely confined to FO pre-training, and extending it to ZO settings is nontrivial due to
the lower quality of ZO gradient estimates. JAGUAR-Muon [32] attempts to apply GO to full-space
ZO momentum, but incurs additional memory and runtime overhead over MeZO [10] and shows
very limited performance gain. This is likely because applying GO to noisy, high-dimensional
ZO estimates amplifies non-informative spectral components, a limitation also confirmed by our
experiments. By contrast, we show that GO becomes effective for ZO optimization only when coupled
with a subspace formulation, i.e., subspace gradient orthogonalization.

H Detailed Experiment Setups
Models and datasets. For LLM experiments, we evaluate Gemma2-2B [48], LlaMA3-8B [43] and
OPT-13B [2] on the SuperGLUE benchmark [44], including the tasks SST-2 [78], RTE [79], CB [80],
21

BoolQ [81], WiC [82] and SQuAD [83]. Following previous literature [10, 18, 19, 25], we randomly
sample 1,000 train samples and 1,000 test samples. We adopt the same prompts as MeZO [10].
For ViT experiments, we evaluate ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/16 [45] fine-tuned on the CIFAR datasets [46].
Data processing and evaluations are based on the well-established ViT pytorch code base1.
Implementation details. Our ZO-Muon, as well as baselines LOZO and SubZero treat model
parameters as matrices instead of vectors. Following Muon [30], we flatten the last three dimensions
of 4D convolutional parameters and treat them as 2D matrices for optimization. Vector parameters,
as well as the input and output layers, are optimized with MeZO.
Hyperparameters. We adopt hyperparameter settings consistent with existing literature to ensure
rigorous benchmarking. For LLMs, we use a batch size of 16 and train for 20,000 steps, utilizing
a constant learning rate without weight decay [10, 18, 25]. The perturbation step size is fixed at
µ = 10−3 based on its demonstrated robustness across tasks [10, 18]. For ViT experiments, we set
the batch size to 64 (CIFAR-10) and 256 (CIFAR-100). The necessity for increased batch sizes on
CIFAR-100 is demonstrated in Fig. 7 and Sec. 5. We also fix the perturbation stepsize µ = 10−3. For
algorithmic hyperparameters (e.g., sparsity for S-MeZO; rank r and interval v for subspace-based
methods like LOZO and SubZero), we strictly follow the original baseline implementations. For
LoRA, we follow previous works [10, 18] and set rank r = 8 and the scaling factor α = 16. Detailed
settings for both ZO and FO methods are listed in Tab. A1.
Computing resources. Experiments are primarily conducted on A6000 GPUs with 48GB memory.
OPT-13B full Adam fine-tuning is run on H100 GPUs with 100GB memory since A6000 does not
have enough memory under our setting.

Projection matrix sampling strategies. We present additional studies on sampling strategies for
the projection matrices used in Subspace-MeZO and ZO-Muon, with results summarized in Tab. A3.
Our default strategy, denoted as “Random,” constructs the projection matrix P ∈ Rm×r via the
QR decomposition of a Gaussian matrix. Alternatively, the “Sketching” strategy applies Gaussian
sketching [42] to past descending directions to capture informative subspace directions, a technique
also utilized in first-order subspace training [41, 84]. Specifically, we maintain a momentum term
M ∈ Rm×n representing the moving average of past ZO gradients. The matrix P is then derived by
performing QR decomposition on MQ, where Q ∈ Rn×r is drawn from a Gaussian distribution. As
shown in Tab. A3, Gaussian sketching does not yield notable performance gains over random sampling
for either Subspace-MeZO or ZO-Muon, yet it increases GPU memory consumption, particularly
for LLMs, due to the requirement of storing the momentum M. In the ViT fine-tuning scenario,
Gaussian sketching exhibits even worse accuracy, likely due to the cumulative bias introduced by
Momentum as it accumulates past ZO gradient estimates rather than preciser FO gradients.

J Limitations
Despite the extensive experiments and the clear performance/efficiency gain of ZO-Muon, our work
has limitations that highlight future directions. First, we focus on memory-efficient fine-tuning, and
extending ZO-Muon to full pretraining of large-scale models remains open. Second, exploring the
application of ZO optimization in other post-training tasks, such as reinforcement learning and safety
alignment, remains open.
