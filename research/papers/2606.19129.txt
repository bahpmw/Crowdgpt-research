Abstract—In decentralized learning, clients train a machine
learning model while keeping their data locally and share their
model parameters or gradients with a set of neighbors, making
it vulnerable to both privacy attacks and model poisoning
attacks. Jointly addressing privacy and Byzantine behavior in
decentralized learning is challenging. Indeed, Differential Pri-
vacy (DP) applied on the client side is known to increase the
variance of honest model updates, which weakens Byzantine-
robust aggregation against poisoning attacks. Distributed DP can
mitigate this issue by adding noise after aggregation, but imple-
menting it without a trusted curator requires both confidential
and Byzantine-resilient computation. Recent work addresses this
challenge using secure multi-party computation (MPC) to imple-
ment robust aggregation while preserving input confidentiality.
However, these solutions scale badly: they either require all-to-
all communication between participants or delegate the entire
computation to a small subset of parties whose computational and
communication cost grows with network size. In this paper, we
present Giskard, a scalable protocol for private and Byzantine-
robust decentralized aggregation. Giskard organizes n parties
into a tree of committees of size O(log n) and evaluates a
differentially private coordinate-wise approximate median via a
committee-adapted secure quantile search over the value domain,
using MPC within each committee. We assess Giskard both
theoretically by proving its security and privacy properties and
empirically through extensive experiments involving up to one
million participants. Compared to its closest competitors, Giskard
reduces per-party communication complexity by close to three
orders of magnitude over the closest competitor while exhibiting
comparable robustness under up to n/4 Byzantine parties.

Secure multiparty computation, Privacy-preserving aggregation
I. INTRODUCTION
Federated learning (FL) was initially promoted as a privacy-
preserving alternative to centralized training, since raw data
remains on users’ devices [1]. However, subsequent work has
shown that FL is vulnerable to a wide range of attacks, many of
which exploit the privileged role of the aggregation server [2].
This centralization also creates a governance bottleneck: the
entity operating the server retains effective control over the
learning process, including the choice of the training objective,
model architecture, aggregation rule. As a result, FL may
decentralize data storage, but it does not decentralize decision-
making. From this perspective, decentralized learning, where
participants interact directly in a peer-to-peer manner [3], [4]
appears to be an appealing alternative as it eliminates the need
for a trusted central all-mighty orchestrator while preserving
the convergence properties of centralized training [5].
Nevertheless, decentralized learning systems face several
challenges that hinder their wider adoption. First, these sys-
tems are inherently vulnerable to Byzantine participants, i.e.,
malicious or faulty nodes that submit arbitrary, manipulated,
or erroneous model updates with the intent to corrupt the
global model [6]. Unlike in FL, where the server can act as a
gatekeeper (albeit a trusted and potentially compromised one),
the absence of a central authority in decentralized settings
makes model poisoning attacks harder to detect and mitigate.
An orthogonal yet equally critical challenge concerns the
privacy of participants’ contributions. Even in the absence of a
central server, nodes must exchange model updates (gradients
or parameter vectors) that can leak sensitive information about
their training data, and such direct access to parties updates,
and the possibility to manipulate them broadens the attack
surface enable privacy attacks potentially stronger than in
FL [7]. Examples of such attacks include membership infer-
ence attacks [8]–[12], where an adversary determines whether
a particular record was used during training and more criti-
cally, gradient/model inversion attacks [13]–[15], whereby an
adversary reconstructs raw training samples with high fidelity
directly from the exchanged gradients and reconstructed ones.
These two challenges have been extensively studied in
isolation, each giving rise to its own line of defenses. On the
one hand, robust aggregation functions have been devised to
deal with Byzantine contributions. These aggregation functions
are designed to limit the influence of malicious updates by
filtering outliers or computing statistics robust to a fraction of
corrupted inputs, such as coordinate-wise Median, Trimmed
Mean, or Krum [16], [17]. These methods offer provable
robustness guarantees up to a bounded fraction of Byzantine
participants. More recently, the challenge of handling model
poisoning attacks within heterogeneous environment was also
tackled by boosting current robust aggregation rule with pre-
aggregation mechanism [18], [19] or by reformulating the loss
function [20]. On the other hand, one of the main protections
against privacy attacks is differential privacy [21] (DP), which
introduces calibrated noise to bound what an adversary can
infer from participants data. However adding noise in the train-
ing process would greatly disrupt model utility. To alleviate
arXiv:2606.19129v2  [cs.CR]  22 Oct 2026
that, a little noise as possible must be added, which can be
done going in the Distributed DP model, where the noise can
be added after aggregating participants updates. This model,
however, requires cryptographic techniques such as Secure
Aggregation [22] or homomorphic encryption [23] to provide
input confidentiality and ensure that individual updates are
never exposed in the clear. So to guarantee end-to-end privacy
while preserving model utility, we must combine differential
privacy with cryptographic protection.
However, it is well known that these two challenges are fun-
damentally in tension: Byzantine-resilient aggregation func-
tions require inspecting and comparing individual contribu-
tions, while privacy mechanisms are designed to hide them.
This conflict limits the applicability of naive combinations.
Approaches that combine robust aggregators with local DP
mechanisms face impossibility results that bound the simul-
taneous guarantees of privacy, robustness, and utility [24].
Alternatively, implementing robust aggregation inside crypto-
graphic protocols is a natural direction, but incurs a heavy
computational and communication cost: aggregation rules such
as Trimmed Mean or Median inherently require sorting and
comparison operations, which are notoriously expensive to
implement in encrypted form.
Prior work has explored MPC-based robust aggregation
in federated settings [25]–[28], where a central coordinator
can structure the computation. Extending these guarantees to
fully decentralized systems is considerably harder. Existing
decentralized proposals [29], [30] involve either all nodes or
a randomly elected committee performing MPC to perform
robust aggregation, without adding a DP mechanism, that
would still open the door to to privacy attacks on the resulting
deciphered aggregate. Furthermore, these designs yield all-
to-all or all-to-committee communication topologies, where
the per-node communication load grows at least linearly with
the number of participants. This constitutes a fundamental
scalability bottleneck that renders them impractical for large-
scale fully decentralized learning deployments, where we may
not expect any of the participant to yield enough computing
and/or networking power to handle such workload.
Our work: In this paper, we aim at bridging this gap by
proposing GISKARD, a scalable, robust and privacy-preserving
decentralized learning system. To reach this objective, we build
on scalable MPC systems designed to withstand malicious ad-
versaries [31], [32] and use them to implement a differentially
private, Byzantine-robust aggregation protocol.
GISKARD distribute secure computation across small com-
mittees to achieve sub-linear per-party communication, while
ensuring that only carefully calibrated noisy statistics are
revealed. Our key technical insight is a new formulation
of coordinate-wise median as a sequence of private secure
counting queries. This formulation enables a lightweight hi-
erarchical aggregation protocol that combines communication
efficiency, differential privacy, and Byzantine resilience.
As a result, GISKARD is a scalable, load-balanced, private
decentralized learning system that tolerates up to n/4 byzan-
tine nodes via a hierarchical aggregation tree, matching the
robustness properties of coordinate-wise median.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose GISKARD, a novel robust and private decen-
tralized learning protocol that scales up to millions of
parties.
• We prove that GISKARD is secure against static malicious
adversaries.
• We analyze GISKARD’s communication cost and show
that the worst per-party communication is polyloga-
rithmic in n while its closest competitors have worst
per-party communication complexity in the order of
O(n log2 n).
• We empirically evaluate GISKARD on four datasets and
at network sizes up to n = 106. GISKARD reduces
worst-case per-party communication by close to three
orders of magnitude over the closest baseline, while
its robust aggregation function matches the utility of
formally robust aggregators.
• The code is publicly available1.

B. Differential Privacy
To analyze formal privacy guarantees in GISKARD, Differ-
ential Privacy (DP) is a standard framework. DP provides a
formal guarantee that the output of a statistical function does
not depend too strongly on the contribution of any individual
client. Sample-level DP protects the addition or removal of a
single record, whereas user-level (or client-level) DP, which we
consider in this paper, protects the addition or removal of all
records associated with a single client [34], [35]. Accordingly,
in user level DP, two datasets D and D′ are neighboring,
denoted D ∼ D′, if one can be obtained from the other
by adding or removing the entire local dataset of a single
party [35].

A randomized mechanism M satisfies (ε, δ)-differential
privacy (DP) if, for any neighboring datasets D ∼ D′ and
any measurable set S,
Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ eε Pr[M(D′) ∈ S] + δ. (2)

In collaborative learning, we distinguish between mecha-

C. Byzantine Robust Collaborative Learning
1) Threat to Collaborative Learning Model: While averag-
ing is the most common aggregation policy in collaborative
learning, it is also the most vulnerable to cases where par-
ties fail to send correct model update. Such failures include
parties that fail to respond, have their updates corrupted by
faults, or adversarially submit crafted updates that bias the
aggregate. We refer to this class of attacks as model poisoning
attacks [38], [39].
2) Defense against Model Poisoning Attacks: The literature
gives a wide range of defenses against model poisoning.
A first class restricts client updates to a bounded domain,
preventing deviations far from the mean [40]. A second class
deterministically detects and excludes corrupted updates from
aggregation [41].
A third class replaces averaging with robust statistical
functions that tolerate a fraction of corrupted inputs. We focus
on this third class, which is supported by the theoretical
framework described below.
Definition 3. (f, κ)-Byzantine robustness [19] Let f = ρn
with ρ < 1 2 and κ ≥ 0, the aggregation rule F is (f, κ)-
Byzantine robust if there exist a value κ ∈ R+ so that for any
(x1, . . . , xn) ∈ RN and any subset S ⊆ [n] of size n-f the
output of F is bounded as follow :
∥F (x1, . . . , xn) − xS ∥2 ≤ κ |S| X i∈S ∥xi − xS ∥2 (5)
where xS = 1 |S| P i∈S xi.
Such an aggregator’s output deviates by at most a bounded
multiple of the honest dispersion, regardless of the attacker
knowledge and attack strategies.
Examples of (f, κ)-Byzantine robust aggregators include
coordinate-wise estimators (trimmed mean and median [16])
and geometry-based aggregators (Krum and Multi-Krum [17],
Geometric Median [42]).
D. Cryptographic Building Blocks
1) Multi-Party Computation: GISKARD relies on secure
multi-party computation (MPC) [43]: a set of parties P =
{1, . . . , n}, each holding a private input xi ∈ I, jointly
compute a public function f : In → On such that party i
learns only its own output fi((xj )n j=1) ∈ O. We instantiate this
with secret-sharing-based MPC in the honest-majority setting,
building on Shamir’s scheme and the BGW verifiable variant.
We recall both schemes below.
2) Verifiable Secret Sharing: Let Fq be a field of size
at least q > n, and let t denote the maximum number of
corrupted MPC parties.
Shamir secret sharing [44] represents a secret s ∈ Fq as
evaluations (denoted shares) of a random polynomial of degree
t whose constant term is s, ensuring that t + 1 share can
recover the secret, while t share reveal no information about
s. A key property is linearity: any addition and multiplication
by a public constant can be performed with local operation on
the shares.
To tolerate malicious dealers and parties we use the BGW
verifiable secret-sharing (VSS) scheme [45], [46]. VSS ensures
that honest parties can reject inconsistent shares or reconstruct
the secret despite malformed ones. BGW provides protocols
for multiplying shared values and for robust reconstruction,
where malformed shares submitted by corrupted parties can
be detected or corrected. These mechanisms protect our MPC
computation against active deviations by up to t < n/3 cor-
rupted parties. For completeness, we provide the full descrip-
tion of the BGW sharing, multiplication, and reconstruction
protocols in Appendix A .
Theorem 1 ( [46]). The BGW protocol is unconditionally
secure against a static malicious adversary corrupting up to
t < n/3 parties, with a straight-line black-box simulator.
E. Seed Agreement and Committee Formation
To make GISKARD scalable, we need to be able to form
committees of a small number of parties that guarantee, with
an extremely high probability, that they contain a sufficient
proportion of honest parties. This requires the robust genera-
tion of a random seed, followed by the use of a public function
so that each party can sample the committee membership. This
is a standard construction that has been extensively studied and
which we therefore reuse [31], [32], based on the composition
of scalable Byzantine Agreement [47]–[49], which gives the
following.
Lemma 1 (Common Reference String Generation). For all
ϵ > 0, there exists a protocol such that for n parties in a
synchronous network with authenticated channels, against a
static adversary corrupting at most (1/4 − ϵ)n parties:
• With probability 1 − n−c for any constant c, all honest
parties agree on a common string seed of length Θ(log n)
with at least a 2/3 + ϵ fraction of uniformly random bits.
• The per-party communication cost is polylog(n) bits.
• The round complexity is polylog(n).
The proof is adapted from composing [47] with [49, Lemma
9]. The protocol is run only once during system setup. Its
output seed deterministically defines the tree topology via the
public random functions. This topology is then reused across
all subsequent operations, so no further BA invocations are
needed per-operation.
III. MODELS AND DESIGN GOALS
We first describe the system and threat models (§III-A–
§III-B) and finally the design goals (§III-C).
A. System and Network Model
We model our collaborative learning system as a set of n
parties, P = {P1, . . . , Pn}. The parties jointly execute a pre-
scribed protocol Π to collaboratively learn a global model. We
assume that the set of parties P consists of two disjoint sets:
the honest parties H, which strictly follow Π, and the adversar-
ial parties A (also called Byzantine parties), with H ∪ A = P
and H ∩ A = ∅. We assume a synchronous communication
network where computation proceeds in rounds and messages
sent by honest parties are delivered within a known bounded
delay. We further assume a public-key infrastructure (PKI)
that authenticates point-to-point communication channels, and
the presence of a secure communication protocol such as
TLS to ensure channel confidentiality, all being necessary pre-
requisite for our MPC primitives.
B. Threat Model
We consider a static, computationally bounded adversary
A who corrupts a set of parties of size f = |A| prior to
protocol execution. Static corruption means that the adversary
choose the corrupted parties, and cannot change this choice
during the protocol execution. The computationally bounded
assumption implies that the adversary is limited to polynomial-
time computation and as such cannot break the underlying
cryptographic assumptions, such as the security of authenti-
cation and encryption mechanisms. The adversarial objective
is to undermine the confidentiality, privacy, and correctness of
the aggregation process.
a) Attacker capabilities.: GISKARD considers a Byzan-
tine threat model in which corrupted parties may arbitrarily
deviate from the prescribed protocol Π. At the protocol level,
corrupted parties may send malformed shares, colluding to
reconstruct shared secrets, or attempting to abort the protocol.
At the model learning level, corrupted parties may submit
adversarially crafted model updates in order to bias the global
aggregated model.
b) Corruption bound.: We require f < n/4. This bounds
results from the requirement for the optimized MULTIPLY
protocol used within GISKARD and the committee formation
requirements.
C. Design Goals
Our primary objective is to ensure secure and reliable
decentralized learning even when a subset of parties acts
maliciously. Specifically, GISKARD is designed to achieve the
following three goals:
(1) Byzantine Robustness. We want our aggregation rule
to be provably robust, ideally under the (f, κ)-robustness
(Def. 3), so that the training process gets robustness guarantee
against model poisoning attacks.
(2) Privacy and Security. GISKARD must provide formal
differential privacy for honest updates, while ensuring perfect,
information-theoretic confidentiality against static malicious
adversaries under the assumed corruption bound. Only pre-
scribed outputs may be revealed, enabling distributed DP
without a trusted aggregator. It must also guarantee output
delivery to all honest parties despite missing, malformed, or
inconsistent adversarial contributions.
(3) Scalability. The per-party communication overhead
must grow sub-linearly in the total number of participants,
ensuring that the protocol remains practical at cross-device
scale, concretely for deployments involving n ≥ 104.
The main challenge is scalability, since robust rules use
costly non-linear operations and processing all n inputs in one
MPC instance concentrates computation and communication.
The rule and its MPC realization must therefore be co-
designed to satisfy Goal (3).

2) Hierarchical Committee Architecture: To distribute the
aggregation workload, GISKARD organizes the parties into
small committees arranged as a k-ary tree of depth L. Figure 1
illustrates this architecture. Each level of the tree has a specific
role:
• Leaves (depicted as Level 0 in Figure 1) – all n
parties. Each compares its local vector to the current pivot
coordinate-wise and secret-shares the resulting bit vector
to its assigned base committee.
• Base committees (depicted as Level 1 in Figure 1) –
each receives shares from n/kL leaves, validates share
domain and well-formedness, sums them, and forwards
re-shared sums to its parent.
• Intermediate committees (depicted as Levels 2 to L−1
in Figure 1) – receive shares from k child committees,
convert and re-aggregate, forward upward.
• Root committee (depicted as Level L in Figure 1)
– performs final checks and aggregation, adds jointly
generated Gaussian noise to the count, reconstructs the
noisy count in the clear, selects the next pivot, and
disseminates it down the tree.
This hierarchical organization distributes the computation
and communication load across multiple committees rather
than concentrating it on a single one. We next presents the
complete GISKARD protocol and shows how these levels
interact during setup and each median-search iteration.

C. Sub-Protocols
We now detail the four sub-protocols invoked by GISKARD
(Algorithm 2): BUILDTREE (line 1), INPUTSHARE (line 6),
RESHARE (line 10) and NOISEGEN (line 14).
1) BUILDTREE for Topology Generation: BUILDTREE is a
one-time protocol that randomly constructs the communication
topology through seed agreement and local assignment. Honest
parties agree on a k-ary tree of depth L, whose leaves are
parties and whose internal nodes are size-m committees with
fewer than m/4 corrupted members. A party may belong to
multiple committees and learns its base committee, its internal
committee memberships, and their members. By Theorem 1,
seed agreement produces an unpredictable string from which
two public pseudorandom functions derive committee mem-
bership and leaf assignment. More details can be found in
Appendix B-A.
2) INPUTSHARE with Domain Proof: After locally compar-
ing its vector coordinates to the current pivot, leaf j secret-
shares the resulting bit vector bj,d = 1[xj,d ≤ pt,d] via
VSS-SHARE to its assigned base committee. A malicious
leaf could share an arbitrary field element instead of a bit,
biasing the aggregated count by an unbounded amount. To
prevent this, the committee verifies that each shared value b
satisfies b ∈ {0, 1} via the standard degree-2 domain proof:
it runs MULTIPLY([b], [1 − b]) and reconstructs the result
using VSS-RECONST. If the reconstruction yields 0, the share
is accepted; otherwise the committee collectively replaces it
with a canonical zero sharing. This bounds the Byzantine
contribution per iteration to CB ∈ [0, f ]. Committee parties
then locally sum the shares, producing a share of the partial
count from the incoming leaves.
3) Verifiable Share Redistribution via RESHARE: RE-
SHARE converts a (τ, m)-Shamir sharing held by a child
committee Cch into a fresh (τ, m)-sharing held by its parent
Cpa. Two adversarial behaviors must be taken into account:
a corrupted child may send sub-shares that are inconsistent
with any degree-τ polynomial, or send consistent share but for
the wrong secret. Both are handled by VSS-SUBSHARE [46,
Prot. 6.8], ensuring that the parent committee receive correct
sub-shares that correspond to the correct held secret. Each
parent reconstructs its share by combining the child commit-
tee’s contributions using Lagrange interpolation (see Fig. 2).
Because every child contribution corresponds to an evaluation
of the original polynomial, this weighted combination pro-
duces an evaluation of a newly randomized polynomial while
preserving the same secret value at its constant term. Thus,
the parent committee receives fresh shares of the value held
by the child committee without revealing that value. The full
protocol is given in Appendix B.

The scalability of GISKARD does not require the aggrega-

median is not associative. Instead, its evaluation must reduce
to a small number of public-query rounds whose answers are
compact associative aggregates. At round t, a public state qt

We identify three requirements for a GISKARD-compatible
realization.
(G1) Compact and query-efficient decomposability: the
rule admits compact mergeable summaries and a non-
combinatorial number of public-query rounds.
(G2) Efficiently certifiable robustness: Analogous to the
computational surjectivity requirement of Franzese et
al. [29], we could enforce that each contribution only ad-
mits a fixed admissible input, while robustness continues
to hold for arbitrary Byzantine choices of such inputs.
(G3) Low secure arithmetic complexity: producing and
merging summaries uses mainly linear operations and few
inexpensive multiplications, avoiding extensive compar-
isons, sorting, divisions, or spectral computations.
These requirements capture complementary bottlenecks:
(G1) determines whether computation can be distributed over
the committee hierarchy, (G2) prevents an unchecked Byzan-
tine interface at the leaves, and (G3) controls the cost within
each committee. For example, the average function is per-
fectly decomposable and inexpensive to aggregate, but an
unrestricted Byzantine summand has unbounded influence and
therefore does not provide Byzantine robustness [17].

V. THEORETICAL ANALYSIS
A. Security Analysis of Giskard
We now state the main security results of GISKARD, the
proofs being provided in the appendix in section C. Our main
argument is presented in a modular approach.
We first assume that BUILDTREE was successfully per-
formed before running the secure median computation, so
that all formed committees respect the byzantine tolerance
bound. We then establishes that all the defined sub-protocols
are secure under the universal composability framework [56].
We finally show GISKARD is perfectly secure using all the
previous results and composition theorem [56].
We first collect the security guarantees of the underlying
cryptographic building blocks.
Lemma 2. Protocols BA, VSS-SHARE, VSS-RECONST,
VSS-SUBSHARE, and MULTIPLY are UC-secure against
static malicious adversaries.
We next state the security guarantees of the sub-protocols
built from these components.
Lemma 3. Protocol INPUTSHARE, RESHARE and
NOISEGEN are UC-secure against static malicious
adversaries.
The proofs are provided separately for each subprotocol
respectively in Appendix C-F, C-C and C-E2. Combining these
results through the UC composition theorem yields the security
of the complete protocol.
Theorem 2. Assuming that BUILDTREE completes success-
fully, protocol GISKARD securely realizes the approximated
coordinate-wise median functionality in the UC framework
against static malicious adversaries, provided that f < n/4.
The proof of Theorem 2 is provided in Appendix C-F.

VI. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate GISKARD on scalability, by comparing its
communication and latency costs with MPC-based decentral-
ized baselines (A2C and A2A), and model utility, by testing
whether its binary-search median formulation remains robust
under Byzantine attacks. We implemented GISKARD in Rust
using arkworks [58], and obtained plaintext baselines with a
modified ByzFL framework in Python [59]. All experiments
ran on an Ubuntu 24.04 workstation with an Intel Core i9-
13950HX CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 5000 GPU. The source
code is available in the Giskard Repository. Our evaluation is
guided by the following research questions:
• RQ1: To what extent does GISKARD reduce per-party
communication cost relative to A2A and A2C topologies?
• RQ2: How does the network size n affect the end-to-end
latency of each topology?
• RQ3: Under which data-heterogeneity regimes does
GISKARD retain robust performance under Byzantine
attacks, and how does it compare with state-of-the-art
plaintext robust aggregators?
• RQ4: How does DP budget affect GISKARD’s resilience
to Byzantine model-poisoning attacks ?

D. Impact of Differential Privacy on Byzantine Robustness
(RQ4)
We next evaluate whether GISKARD can provide DP while
preserving Byzantine resilience. Because aggregation occurs
inside MPC, noise is added after secure aggregation while
under Local DP, each update is perturbed beforehand, increas-
ing the dispersion of honest updates. To isolate this statistical
effect from MPC costs, we emulate both privacy mechanisms
in plaintext while retaining their respective privacy accounting.
For GISKARD, we compose zCDP additively across noisy
count queries and convert the resulting guarantee to (ϵ, δ)-DP.
For the Local DP baselines, we independently add Gaussian
noise to each party’s update before Median or CAF and
account for the privacy budget using Opacus [60] and Renyi
Differential Privacy [61]. We compare all mechanisms at the
same final (ϵ, δ) privacy guarantees, with δ = 10−5.
Figure 6 display the global test accuracy of model trained
with GISKARD against Local DP baselines (using Median and
CAF aggregators) on 12 settings encompassing 4 attacks and
3 privacy budgets. On non-i.i.d EMNIST dataset with α = 1.0
and n = 11850, GISKARD achieve the highest accuracy
accross 11 of the 12 experiment settings. Under Sign Flipping,
it remains near 91% across all privacy budgets, outperforming
both local-DP baselines by approximately 56% at ϵ = 10 and
3% at ϵ = 100. Its advantage is largest under FoE: at ϵ = 100,
GISKARD achieves 63.1%, versus 16.0% for Local DP+CAF
and 10% accuracy for Local DP+Median. These results high-
light GISKARD’s distributed-DP advantage. Although CAF
is more robust under extreme plaintext heterogeneity, Local
DP perturbs honest updates before aggregation. By enabling
central-DP-style noise placement without a trusted curator,
GISKARD avoids this distortion and can outperform Local DP
even when combined with with CAF.

VII. RELATED WORK
Early privacy-preserving Byzantine-robust FL systems often
combined separate mechanisms for robustness and privacy.
Biscotti [62] combines Multi-Krum and DP noise with se-
cure aggregation, but performs robust aggregation in plain-
text. BREA [63] uses VSS-based MPC to compute pairwise
distances, while the server receives the distance matrix in
the clear to complete Multi-Krum. Similarly, SHARE [64]
securely aggregates random client clusters but applies robust
aggregation in plaintext to the resulting cluster means. Thus,
those approaches still expose intermediate information to the
server, resulting in inherent privacy leakage. A subsequent
line of work focuses on combining robust aggregation with
DP. Allouah et al. [24] show that achieving a meaningful DP
guarantee from local DP on top of (f, κ)-robust aggregation
sharply degrades model utility. CafCor [55] tackle this trade-
off by replacing local DP with a correlated-noise mechanism
across parties, and introducing a new (f, κ)-robust aggregator
built on a covariance-bound-agnostic filter. It nonetheless
operates in a classical FL setting with an honest-but-curious
aggregation server.
A second line of work implements robust aggregation
rules via cryptographic primitives in server-based settings.
Sable [25] uses somewhat-homomorphic-encryption operators
to compute trimmed-mean, but relies on a single powerful
and honest-but-curious server. AlphaFL [26] and EIFFeL [65]
incorporate heuristic input validation into secure aggregation
rather than formally robust rules under multi-server or client-
server setups. Building on EIFFeL, DP-BREM+ [66] combines
zero-knowledge input validation with MPC-based distributed
noise generation to provide DP guarantee. However, it remains
restricted FL cross-silo topologies and exhibits per-client com-
munication scaling linearly with model dimension, system size
and adversary bounds.
Removing the server assumption shifts robust aggregation
assumptions entirely, forcing a stricter threat model. Se-
cureDL [30] use a maliciously secure MPC framework to
implement a cosine-similarity filtering rule for aggregation.
However, its all-to-all topology scales poorly while cosine-
similarity filtering offers no formal robustness guarantee.
Franzese et al. [29] formalize robust serverless collabora-
tive learning in an all-to-committee MPC setting, using a
lightweight robust mechanism. Their single-layer topology
concentrates computation on a small committee, giving per-
party costs close to multi-server approaches, while the absence
of DP leaves the global model vulnerable to privacy attacks.
GISKARD addresses these gaps by evaluating a formally robust
aggregator via hierarchical MPC committees and distributed
DP, scaling robust decentralized learning to large networks
with per-party sub-linear communication overhead.
VIII. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented GISKARD, a scalable, robust,
and privacy-preserving decentralized learning system that
addresses the per-party communication bottleneck of exist-
ing approaches. In fully decentralized architecture, GISKARD
achieves polylogarithmic per-party communication while com-
bining Byzantine resilience and differential privacy in a ma-
licious threat model. We establish its security and robustness
guarantees, analyze its communication and practical costs and
empirically show that it is competitive at scale. As future
work, we plan to investigate non-interactive proof techniques
to further reduce communication costs.

C. VSS-RECONST
We now describe the BGW reconstruction of a secret
protocol as described in [46, Step 3 of Prot. 7.1]. Suppose
that a secret s ∈ F is shared via Shamir Secret Sharing using
a polynomial f of degree t such that f (0) = s, and that each
party Pi possesses a share f (ai). Normally, if all participants
are honest, they can broadcast their shares, and ultimately t+1

we are in a malicious scenario, and in this case, we must
guard against the possibility that some of the shares used to
reconstruct the secret may be incorrect, which would skew the
final result. Therefore, a mechanism is needed to efficiently
identify and correct these incorrect shares.
We know here that all the shares contains atleast n ≥ 3t + 1
points lying in the same polynomial containing secret s. As
such, it is possible to use an efficient Reed-Solomon decoding
algorithm such as Berlekamp–Welch to recover the exact
polynomial to get the secret .

D. VSS-SUBSHARE
We now describe the BGW subsharing protocol as described
in [46, Prot. 6.8]. The VSS-SUBSHARE protocol enables
verifiable resharing of existing Shamir shares. Suppose a set of
parties holds a secret s distributed into shares via a polynomial
f, such that each party Pi holds bi = f (ai). Then each party
Pi generates a random polynomial of degree t, gi, such that
gi(0) = bi = f (ai). Here, shares are created based on the
shares of the common secret.
The challenge here is to figure out how to prevent a
malicious party from sending evaluations corresponding to a
polynomial whose constant term is gi(0) ̸= f (ai). To do this,
we exploit the supermajority property of the honest parties
and can therefore represent the vector of the constant parts
of the generated polynomials as a Reed-Solomon codeword
γ = (g1(0), . . . , gn(0)) that differs from the valid codeword
β = (f (a1), . . . , f (an)) by only τ values, making it possible
to use a code correction algorithm. We can then write the
difference between these two vectors as γ = β + e, where
e is an error vector of maximum weight τ. Let H be the
parity-check matrix of the corresponding Reed-Solomon code.
Since β is a valid codeword, we have βH T = 0, and
by multiplying the expression for γ by H T , we obtain the
syndrome s = γH T = (β + e)H T = 0 + eH T .
The error-correcting algorithm can therefore recover this
error vector from the syndrome, and thus directly identify
the erroneous segments. In particular, for a vector e, all
indices i such that ei ̸= 0 correspond to the indices of the
parts that have cheated, and whose evaluations must there-
fore be discarded and replaced, directly by setting f (ai) =
gi(0) − ei. It is therefore necessary to retrieve this syn-
drome s in addition to the subshares, which is ensured
securely by the Fmat protocol. Specifically, after sending
the subshares via VSS-SHARE, each party i ends up with
a subshare vector (g1(ai), . . . , gn(ai)). The parties i can
then calculate locally, using the public matrix H T , Y (ai) =
(g1(ai), . . . , gn(ai))H T , corresponding to the evaluation at ai
of the vector of 2t polynomials of degree t, Y (x), whose
constant term is Y (0) = (g1(0), . . . , gn(0))H T = γH T = s.
Revealing this polynomial thus allows the syndrome to be re-
covered; this is achieved by having the parties i broadcast their
evaluation of Y (x) and using VSS-RECONST to reconstruct
the syndrome.
Once the syndrome is obtained, the index j of the cheating
parties is identified, and for those parties, the corresponding
subshares are broadcast to reconstruct gj (0), which in turn
allows the correct original share f (aj ) = gj (0) − ej to be
recovered.
Note that in the version described in [46], the VSS-
SUBSHARE protocol takes place within a single commit-

takes place between two committees. In this case, the source
committee will only subshare its shares as the dealer via
VSS-SHARE, and it is up to the receiving committee to
perform the syndrome calculation and error correction. Note
that, given the properties of our committees (same size, same
corruption bound), this modified protocol does not alter the
algebraic properties of the sent shares in any way; therefore,
the syndrome calculation remains the same as if the dealers
were from the same committee, and thus the argument for
