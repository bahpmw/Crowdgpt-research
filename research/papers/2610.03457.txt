Cross-Facility LLM Pre-training on HPC:
Elastic Aggregation, Data Leasing, and Queue-Aware Placement
Zarè Palanciyan1, Thomas van Osch1, Douwe van der Wal1, Olivera Kotevska2, and Tim
Kok1
1SURF, Amsterdam, The Netherlands
2Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN, USA
Abstract
Academic compute is fragmented: allocations are granted per facility, and facilities differ in accelerators
and software stacks, schedule jobs independently, and share neither a network nor a filesystem. We present
a system that pools such allocations to pre-train a single language model across three supercomputers
on two continents, up to 7,400km apart: Snellius (NVIDIA H100), LUMI and Frontier (both AMD
MI250X). It combines (i) DiLoCo-style two-loop training with an elastic, token-weighted Nesterov outer
step for which zero, one or many live sites are all normal states; (ii) DARL, a data-leasing protocol whose
heartbeat-backed leases guarantee that, within an epoch, no sample is trained twice or lost under crashes,
late joins and work stealing; and (iii) queue-aware placement driven by unprivileged sbatch --test-only
probes. Training Qwen3-0.6B on C4 for 20,000 optimiser steps, the three-site run reaches a held-out
perplexity of 34.7, against 28.2 for a centralised baseline. Per-round overhead (weight exchange and
checkpointing) stays near 110s regardless of the number of local steps H, so its share of wall-clock time
falls from 32% at H=100 to 5.7% at H=1,000 and 3.1% at H=2,000. In a 23.8h three-site run with
four site departures, 1.2% of granted data blocks were reclaimed and none was duplicated or lost. In an
idealised queue-model projection, queue-aware placement shortens time-to-target by 18–43% compared
with waiting for all sites to be allocated. Cross-site pre-training is thus operational rather than competitive:
it turns fragmented allocations into one training run at a measured cost.
1 Introduction
Training frontier-grade foundation models in an acceptable time span requires more capacity than a single
supercomputer in the public research sector provides. The individual allocations that high-performance
computing (HPC) systems currently award are not enough to train a language model. Researchers must
therefore aggregate capacity across national, regional and leadership-class facilities. That capacity is
fragmented, with allocation divided over different facilities, each with different hardware, a separate queue,
and its own expiry (e.g., the 19 AI Factories and 13 AI Factory Antennas established by the European
Union[1]). Academic groups must combine those pieces into one training run and not a series of unrelated
jobs. This is the practical obstacle that researchers must overcome to train models at the scale of industry.
We therefore ask what an effective distributed training system looks like in a real deployment on public
research infrastructure. Such a system must solve three problems simultaneously. The first is training logistics:
how sites divide the corpus and exchange weights when they share neither a filesystem nor a network. The
second is metascheduling: how to coordinate independent batch jobs at different facilities, when none can start
on demand. The third is infrastructure: every system brings its own accelerators, software stack, connectivity
rules, and site policy. Leave any one unsolved, and the run does not start, does not finish, or does not
converge.
The setup for this experiment is training a single 0.6B-parameter language model on disjoint shards of
a dataset without repetition distributed across LUMI [17, 18], Frontier [9] and Snellius [24]. Through the
novel distributed allocation and remote leasing (DARL) leasing protocol, the run is flexible in late joins and
scheduled churns. We also report a comparison against centralised training. Our contributions are as follows:

2 Related work
Low-communication and federated optimisation. Local stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and its
descendants [25, 16] established that replicas may take many local steps between synchronisations. DiLoCo [6]
scaled the recipe to language models with an inner AdamW loop and an outer Nesterov step on averaged
deltas. OpenDiLoCo [13] and INTELLECT-1 [12] then demonstrated geo-distributed runs at increasing
scale. The aggregation itself descends from FedAvg [19] and its server-side-momentum variants [10, 21, 11].
Photon [22] reported that federated pre-training beat a centralised baseline. At matched tokens, however, its
federated configuration takes roughly 8× more optimiser steps, with local batches with higher local learning
rates than centralised. The comparison with a federation with a different optimisation regime is therefore
improper. Our campaign matches tokens and steps and the combined per-step batch, which removes that
mechanism by design. The two studies answer different questions.
Geo-distributed systems and queue-aware placement. Other work treats cross-site training as a
systems and scheduling problem. Measurements of training on spot VMs across several continents show that
the latency between continents and the ratio of compute to communication limit how well it scales, and that
data-transfer fees can exceed the cost of the VMs [8]. An analytical model shows that data parallelism across
cloud regions slows down sharply, while pipeline parallelism keeps its throughput but costs more in transfer
fees [26]. DT-FM schedules training over decentralised GPUs joined by slow links [30]. Sailor chooses how
many GPUs of each type to use in each cloud zone and re-plans when their availability changes [27]. None of
these works models the batch queue through which HPC systems run every job.
Fairshare and backfill set start times, and walltime caps bound how long any replica can live. A job
that ends is not preempted but simply finished. The relevant literature is therefore queue-wait and runtime
prediction from scheduler history [23, 4, 28], which targets the placement of independent jobs within a single
facility. Only recently has that signal reached training itself. xFFL orchestrates federated runs across facilities
through a workflow manager [5]. FedQueue predicts per-facility admission delays and absorbs them inside
the aggregation rule, buffering late arrivals and down-weighting stale updates [14].
A field-tested LLM pre-training setup with elastic aggregation, dynamic data-leasing protocol, queue-aware
distributed job allocation separates current work from existing literature.
3 System
3.1 Two-loop training and elastic aggregation
Following DiLoCo, each site i runs H=100 inner AdamW steps from the current global model w(t), then
uploads its delta ∆(t) i = θ(t) i −w(t) together with the token count it consumed. With token shares pi, the
server applies an outer Nesterov step
v(t+1) =w(t) +η∑ i pi∆(t) i , w(t+1) =v(t+1) +β ( v(t+1) −v(t)) , (1)
with η=0.7, β=0.9 [6]; our implementation is pinned in tests against torch.optim.SGD with Nesterov
momentum to 2 × 10−6. Setting β=0,η=1 recovers exact FedAvg. Three changes to Eq. 1 make the outer
optimisation loop elastic, so sites can join or leave at any round. First, a round with a single live contributor
takes the full step (η=1), instead of discarding 1 −η of its update. Second, every delta records the merge
2
round it was derived from, and the server rejects stale generations, a requeued job that trained from an old
global model cannot poison the average. Third, the authoritative global step advances by the maximum steps
any contributor took. Weights cross the WAN as bfloat16 (1.32GiB per message for this model), inline in the
gRPC message below the 2GiB cap and streamed tensor-by-tensor through the blob store above it, a small
HTTP PUT/GET service on the same VM. For sites reachable only via an HTTP forward proxy that does
not permit long-lived gRPC streams, the same round protocol runs exclusively over short HTTP requests.
3.2 Distributed Allocation and Remote Leasing (DARL)
Clusters must not train on overlapping samples, and no sample may be lost when a node fails. A window
trained twice in an epoch is counted twice in the token-weighted average of Eq. 1, tilting the merged model
toward whichever site duplicated it; a window dropped when a site vanishes shrinks the epoch with no record
that it did. Heterogeneous sites also drain data at different rates and their availability is asynchronous, so any
static partition is wasteful. DARL is a dynamic data-leasing protocol: a central coordinator holds the only
authoritative map of which data blocks are unassigned, leased, or committed, and clusters lease contiguous
blocks under heartbeat-backed time-to-live (TTL) contracts.
Every site holds an identical pre-tokenised dataset D of N sample windows. The coordinator partitions D
into M = ⌈N/K⌉ blocks of K consecutive samples; K trades statistical mixing against I/O efficiency, since
one block is one contiguous read. Because corpora such as C4 are not shuffled on disk, assigning block ranges
naively would give each site a different topical slice of the corpus and induce drift between the site models, so
DARL shuffles at block level with a deterministic global permutation πs of the M blocks, generated from a
shared seed s by the Durstenfeld variant of Fisher-Yates [7]. A digest of (N,K,s) is checked at registration,
such that two sites cannot silently join with different block spaces. Lease granularity matches the DiLoCo
inner phase, which consumes H·B·W·A samples (H inner steps, per-rank batch B, W ranks, A accumulation
steps): Mphase = max

Leases expire on a TTL of α ¯tstep + βRoundTripTime (with α=2.5) refreshed by heartbeats, so a crashed
site’s untouched data returns to the pool automatically; a drained site steals the untouched tail of the largest
outstanding lease; and grants are proportionally sized to each site’s measured commit rate, so fast sites are
neither starved nor hoarding. Exactly-once coverage is an ordering guarantee: a site checkpoints before it
commits blocks, so its weights and its data-consumption record can only fail together.
3.3 Scheduling
Placement across facilities is a question no batch scheduler answers: each site schedules only its own machine,
and prior cross-facility systems (§2) hide admission delay rather than choose where to submit. Choosing
requires queue-time estimates alongside each site’s walltime cap and per-GPU throughput, and obtaining
them is the hard part on production HPC, where most sites expose no scheduler API and do not permit
cronjobs. All three of our sites run Slurm, so a single unprivileged mechanism suffices.
Observing queue times. For each site c we define a set of job shapes Sc; a shape fixes a partition, node
count, GPUs per node and walltime. Every ⟨∆⟩ the collector runs sbatch --test-only per shape, placing
the job against the live queue without enqueuing it. Slurm assumes every queued job runs to its requested
walltime and is therefore systematically pessimistic; we discount each estimate by ⟨γ⟩, derived from realized
walltime usage in a trailing sacct window.
Planning submissions. Work is expressed in data units, an arbitrary quantum of which one outer round
consumes ⟨R⟩; each site carries a per-GPU rate from ⟨historical job logs⟩. A waterfill [3] assigns outstanding
units to the soonest-available site to minimise predicted round completion, splitting proportionally to
throughput when several are free and capping each job at the site’s walltime limit and optionally adding
reserve time to compensate for other sites starting later. Parameters: a minimum units per job, the discount
⟨γ⟩, reserve ⟨ρ⟩, and a maximum probe age beyond which a site is excluded. The plan is recomputed every
⟨∆⟩, so newly free capacity is folded in without disturbing running jobs. Misprediction costs calendar time
only: a job that starts late leases no shards (§3.2), so no work is lost or duplicated and the reserve ⟨ρ⟩ can
dampen the impact.

5 Conclusion
In this paper we have described a system for LLM pre-training across three heterogeneous supercomputers
differing in hardware, network connectivity, and batch-scheduling policy. On top of DiLoCo, it introduces
elastic aggregation, DARL, and queue-aware planning to provide the flexibility and resilience that batch-
scheduled infrastructure requires. After 20,000 optimiser steps, the three-site run reaches a held-out perplexity
of 34.7, against 28.2 for a centralised baseline. At H = 1000 only 6% measured communication overhead.
Data coverage held throughout: across four site departures in a three-site run, 1.2% of granted blocks were
reclaimed and no block was lost or trained twice. The value of cross-site training today is therefore operational
rather than competitive: it turns fragmented allocations into one training run, at an overhead we can quantify.
Limitations. This was a proof of concept at a single small model scale, one epoch, and one site topology,
without downstream benchmarking. Future work should test production-scale training across more facilities,
with stronger fault tolerance and a planner informed by historical queue traces rather than only live probes.
