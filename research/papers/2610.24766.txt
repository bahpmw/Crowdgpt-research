Abstract
Existing Multi-token prediction (MTP) methods are auxiliary by design: the multi-
horizon loss mostly trains modules attached to the backbone, and each added horizon
costs another module and another pass. We propose Efficient Causal Self-Recursion
(ECHO), which removes both costs. The backbone’s own final layer predicts every
horizon, so the supervision lands on the parameters that generate the next token,
and Dynamic Depth Sampling (DDS) keeps deeper horizons affordable by drawing
the training depth with probabilities that decay along depth. Future prediction thus
becomes part of the autoregressive objective rather than a task attached to it, and the
gains follow on both sides of it. In pretraining, added depth converts into downstream
accuracy where auxiliary modules saturate, concentrating on reasoning and code at
half the MTP training overhead of training every depth. At inference, the pretrained
model is its own drafter: it drafts beyond the depth it was trained for, and keeps its
draft-target alignment on the open-ended, high-entropy generation where post-hoc
drafters lose theirs. The advantage survives a doubled token budget, a repeated
pretraining run, and supervised finetuning.

1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) (Achiam et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a)
are predominantly trained with the Next-Token Prediction (NTP) objective. While NTP has demonstrated
remarkable scalability and effectiveness, it provides supervision only at the immediate next-token horizon,
leaving potentially useful future information unexploited. Moreover, autoregressive decoding remains
inherently sequential, resulting in limited inference throughput.
To better utilize future information, recent work has explored Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) as an auxiliary
objective on top of NTP. By introducing additional future-token prediction targets, MTP provides denser
supervision during training and can generate draft tokens for speculative decoding, enabling inference
acceleration.
Early MTP methods predict multiple future positions independently from the same hidden state Gloeckle
et al. (2024), providing denser supervision but ignoring causal dependencies among future tokens. Subse-
quent causal MTP methods such as DeepSeek-V3 Liu et al. (2024a) address this limitation by introducing
autoregressive dependencies among future prediction steps, significantly improving prediction quality
and improving the backbone model’s overall performance Liu et al. (2024a); Blakeman et al. (2025); Zeng
et al. (2026).
However, both causal and parallel MTP remain auxiliary by construction: future-token prediction
is carried out by dedicated modules attached to the backbone, rather than being integrated into the
autoregressive generation process itself. This leads to two limitations:
1. Indirect Multi-Horizon Supervision
MTP losses primarily supervise the auxiliary prediction modules. Consequently, the backbone
model receives multi-horizon learning signals only indirectly, potentially limiting their impact
on long-horizon planning.
2. Costly Long-Horizon Supervision
Denser supervision requires deeper prediction horizons, yet each additional horizon adds a
prediction module in memory and another pass in compute. This cost limits the prediction
horizon in large-scale causal MTP pretraining, typically to a single draft token.
To address the above limitations, we introduce Efficient Causal Self-Recursion (ECHO), a causal self-
recursive framework that reuses the backbone’s final layer for future prediction and samples per-step
prediction depths with decaying probabilities to reduce the amortized MTP training overhead.
Final-layer parameter sharing ensures that multi-horizon losses directly shape the backbone, rather than
being absorbed by horizon-specific auxiliary modules. As shown in Figure 3a, this allows ECHO to
benefit from deeper supervision: its downstream performance continues to improve at depth 3, whereas
DeepSeek’s MTP (Liu et al., 2024a) does not, consistent with observations in MiMo (LLM-Core Xiaomi,
2025). More fundamentally, training the same parameters responsible for next-token generation to predict
multiple future tokens encourages the backbone to anticipate how the sequence unfolds over subsequent
steps, as evidenced by ECHO’s substantial gains on reasoning tasks.
To make long-horizon supervision computationally efficient, we introduce Dynamic Depth Sampling (DDS),
which samples prediction depths with decaying probabilities. This simultaneously reduces the amortized
MTP training overhead and mitigates the impact of increasing prediction errors and supervision noise at
greater depths. After supervised finetuning, ECHO’s advantage grows further.
Beyond its pretraining benefits, ECHO entirely eliminates the need for post-hoc drafter finetuning.
DDS makes longer-horizon supervision affordable, while final-layer parameter sharing enables depth
extrapolation: since the same shared layer is reused at every draft step, ECHO can extend drafting beyond
the trained prediction depth while maintaining high acceptance rates, at an amortized MTP training
overhead of only 1.5 depth-1 steps (see Section 4.1). By drafting directly with its pretrained weights,
ECHO avoids the overfitting to academic benchmarks associated with post-hoc drafter finetuning (Liu
et al., 2026), yielding substantial acceptance-length gains in realistic, high-entropy generation settings as
shown in Figure 3b.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce Efficient Causal Self-Recursion (ECHO), which predicts every future horizon by
recursively applying the backbone’s own final layer, with the per-step prediction depth drawn
from a distribution decaying along depth.
• ECHO imposes direct multi-horizon supervision, beating auxiliary MTP on reasoning and code
at matched depth while holding on QA & multiple choice, and widening its lead on both classes

2 Related Work
2.1 Multi-Token Prediction
Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) augments next-token prediction with future-token targets, improving
language modeling while providing draft tokens for speculative decoding. It inherits the architecture of
speculative draft models and differs in the training regime: draft models are finetuned post-hoc against a
frozen backbone on a limited dataset, whereas MTP modules are optimized jointly with the backbone
throughout pretraining.
Parallel MTP predicts multiple future positions independently from several output heads on a shared
backbone Gloeckle et al. (2024), following Medusa Cai et al. (2024). Causal MTP instead follows EAGLE Li
et al. (2024): DeepSeek-V3 Liu et al. (2024a) feeds dedicated prediction modules the previous hidden
state together with shifted token embeddings, preserving autoregressive dependencies among future
steps, and GLM-5 Zeng et al. (2026) shares those parameters across all depths, as EAGLE does, to reduce
memory cost.
2.2 Post-hoc Drafter
Post-hoc drafters are trained on top of a frozen target model by finetuning on target-generated responses,
and differ mainly in how a block of draft tokens is produced. Medusa Cai et al. (2024) attaches parallel
heads that draft every position from the final hidden state, while EAGLE-3 Li et al. (2025) re-enters
a lightweight decoder once per drafted token, conditioned on target hidden states, preserving token-
to-token dependency. Recent parallel drafters build on DFlash Chen et al. (2026), which predicts the
whole block in one pass, with DSpark Cheng et al. (2026) restoring intra-block dependency through a
lightweight causal head.
Two limitations are nonetheless inherent to post-hoc drafting. First, the drafter is narrowly aligned with
the target, being finetuned on a dataset orders of magnitude smaller than the pretraining corpus. Second,
scaling that training to the pretraining corpus is compute-heavy from the multi-step or multi-layer
overhead, which has not been shown to improve the backbone’s downstream performance and may even

2.3 Looped Transformers
Looped transformers reuse a shared block of layers across iterations, buying computational depth without
new parameters: parameter efficiency (Lan et al., 2020), adaptive computation (Dehghani et al., 2019),
reasoning at LLM scale (Saunshi et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), test-time compute scaling (Geiping et al.,
2025), and depth allocated per token (Bae et al., 2025). In all of them recursion refines the same token
position under a next-token objective; ECHO instead recurses along the horizon, each step conditioning
on the next future token to predict one position further ahead.
Yet both buy structure rather than recall, and for looping that is a trade: at matched parameters and
compute it leads on reasoning but trails on perplexity and closed-book QA (Saunshi et al., 2025), and at
scale its gain is credited to knowledge manipulation rather than capacity, measured against external dense
families rather than a same-recipe baseline (Zhu et al., 2025). ECHO pays no such price: mathematics,
reasoning and code gain while multiple choice and closed-book QA hold (Figure 3a), and both classes
gain after finetuning (Figure 4b). Looping, latent chain-of-thought (Hao et al., 2025) and ECHO thus price
latent computation differently: repeated passes over one position, annotated chains, and extra positions
— the one price that accepted tokens can repay.
3 ECHO: Efficient Causal Self-Recursion
We first show that final-layer sharing benefits the backbone through direct multi-horizon supervision,
and then dynamic depth sampling makes it affordable at greater depth.
3.1 Direct Multi-Horizon Supervision via Final-Layer Sharing
We show that reusing the backbone’s final layer turns multi-horizon supervision into a direct training
signal: downstream performance improves over auxiliary MTP, and shows further gains at greater depth.
Causal Self-Recursion: we follow the autoregressive design of DeepSeek-V3 (Liu et al., 2024a) to
preserve causal dependency, where each additional horizon is predicted from the previous final-layer
hidden state hL t−1 and the embedding e(yt) of the previously predicted token. As shown in Figure 2,
rather than attaching a dedicated module for each depth, we recursively apply the backbone’s final
decoder layer TL(·) and output head at every horizon, connected to the previous step by a lightweight
fusion layer F(·):

We show two benefits of the resulting direct multi-horizon supervision:
Sharing improves downstream performance, especially in reasoning capability. To isolate the effect
of sharing, we compare ECHO with DeepSeek’s MTP (Liu et al., 2024a) at matched prediction depth
(Figure 3a). Under this comparison, sharing the final layer gives ECHO the lead at depth 1, most clearly on
math, reasoning and code (+0.79, +0.57 and +1.35), and it reaches +2.63 on reasoning and +2.23 on code
at depth 3. The difference lies in where multi-horizon supervision mainly lands. With auxiliary modules,
the signal is largely absorbed by the modules dedicated to individual horizons, leaving the backbone’s
hidden layers specialized for the next token (Mehra et al., 2025). Sharing removes this bottleneck by
construction: since the final layer predicts every horizon, supervision from all horizons is concentrated
on the parameters that generate the next token.
Sharing promotes longer-horizon supervision. To examine how each design uses added depth, we
compare each family with itself from depth 1 to depth 3. Averaged over all fourteen benchmarks, deeper
supervision improves ECHO by 0.63 points, whereas DeepSeek’s MTP gains only 0.16, and ECHO’s
advantage on reasoning and code persists as depth increases (Figure 3a). This divergence follows from
where each new horizon’s loss goes: in ECHO it reaches the backbone directly, whereas in DeepSeek’s
MTP it mostly trains the newly added module, as discussed above. This explains why auxiliary MTP
rarely goes deeper in practice: DeepSeek-V3 trains with a single MTP module (Liu et al., 2024a), and
MiMo finds that adding MTP layers brings no further improvement (LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2025).

4 Pushing the Frontier of Speculative Decoding under Realistic Serving
We analyze how ECHO’s end-to-end paradigm intrinsically aligns the drafter with the target, entirely
eliminating the need for post-hoc drafter finetuning.

4.2 Staying Aligned under realistic setting
We then show that ECHO outperforms existing post-hoc drafters, with larger gains on high-entropy,
realistic, and open-ended tasks, thanks to two complementary factors: causal drafting ensures consistency
among draft tokens, while joint pretraining aligns the draft distribution with the target.
Causal Drafting Matters in Open-Ended Generation. As shown in Figure 3b, ECHO and other causal
drafters outperform parallel drafters on high-entropy tasks, consistent with Liu et al. (2026); Figure 8
shows the same ordering in decode speedup. When the next tokens are uncertain, the target’s choice
is hard to anticipate, so the probability of acceptance falls off steeply with depth and most of a long
draft is discarded. A causal drafter adapts to this by extending its draft step by step, while a parallel
drafter emits a fixed-length block whose deeper tokens are mostly rejected at verification. The balance
shifts on simple code and mathematics benchmarks such as HumanEval, MBPP, and GSM8K, where
long stretches of output are determined by syntax or derivation, and block-parallel drafting becomes
worthwhile (Liu et al., 2026), whereas realistic software engineering tasks such as SWE-bench remain
high-entropy and still favor causal drafting (Figure 3b). Such simple tasks, however, are a small fraction
of real-world usage, account for under 8% of consumer ChatGPT messages, while open-ended practical
guidance, information seeking, and writing account for nearly 80% (Chatterji et al., 2025).
Joint Pretraining Closes the Alignment Gap. Acceptance depends on how closely the draft distribution
matches the target’s (Leviathan et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024), and a drafter can better match the target
where it has seen the target’s data. ECHO is trained with the target end-to-end and sees its full pretraining
corpus. In contrast, auxiliary drafters are fit to a frozen target on a small finetuning set, so their acceptance
drops under distribution shift (Liu et al., 2024b; Hong et al., 2025). The EAGLE-3 pair in Figure 3b
isolates this effect: with architecture and finetuning data held fixed, warm-started EAGLE-3 achieves a
longer accepted length than its cold-start counterpart, consistent with recent findings that pretrained
initialization strengthens drafters, most on out-of-domain inputs (Bie et al., 2026). Yet on open-ended tasks,
warm-starting still falls short of ECHO, with the largest gap on SWE-bench. ECHO is instead optimized
jointly with the target on every pretraining update, so its alignment is maintained throughout training, in
line with the high acceptance rates reported for MTP modules trained jointly during pretraining (Liu
et al., 2024a; LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2025).

We finally examine whether ECHO’s advantage holds along three axes: more pretraining data, deeper
sampling, and repeated runs.

5.1 Scaling with Data
ECHO’s advantage persists with more pretraining data. We extend pretraining of ECHO-1 and
DeepSeek’s MTP-1 from step 78k to step 156k, doubling the token budget. Both models improve, and
ECHO-1 keeps its lead over DeepSeek’s MTP-1 measured at the same step: +0.52 at step 78k and +0.50 at
step 156k, and on reasoning & code it is +0.88 and +0.89 (Figure 4c, Table 2). This indicates that ECHO’s
advantage is stable under data scaling rather than an early-training effect that more data would erase.
5.2 Scaling with Depth
ECHO’s advantage holds across sampling depths. We next train ECHO-2(7), which samples D over
{1, . . . , 7} with E[D] = 2, at 4/3 the MTP training overhead of ECHO-1.5(3). It trails ECHO-1.5(3) in
pretraining but overtakes it after SFT by 0.49, reaching the largest lead over DeepSeek’s MTP-Instruct of
any configuration (+2.10; Table 3). The gap in pretraining may be due to errors accumulating at deeper
positions, which makes their supervision noisier and draws training away from the reliable shallow
depths. After SFT, however, the deeper supervision appears to pay off, possibly because finetuning
makes better use of what the backbone learned.
5.3 Stability across Runs
ECHO’s lead is robust to training and evaluation noise. A reported gap can come from noise rather
than the method itself. We eliminate evaluation noise by decoding greedily, so the remaining source is
training. To measure it, we pretrain ECHO-1 and DeepSeek’s MTP-1 a second time under the same recipe
and compare the two runs at the same step. Across the two runs, ECHO leads DeepSeek’s MTP-1 by
+0.52 and +0.78, and its lead on reasoning & code shifts by only 0.11 (Figure 4c; Section D). ECHO’s
overall and reasoning & code leads are therefore reproducible across runs.
6 Experiment Setup
Full architecture, prompt, scoring and speculative decoding details are given in Section A.
Models and Baselines: Every configuration shares a 10B-A1B Latent MoE backbone (Blakeman et al.,
2025) with 24 layers, multi-head latent attention (Liu et al., 2024a), and 256 routed experts with eight
active per token (Dai et al., 2024), trained under the same recipe so that the comparison isolates the MTP
design.
Pretraining: 600B tokens at sequence length 4,096 and batch size 1,920 in bfloat16 with Muon (Jordan
et al., 2024), on 24 nodes of eight H800 GPUs.
Dynamic Depth Sampling: ECHO-1.5(3) samples D over {1, 2, 3} with π = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1), giving E[D] =
1.5 and ECHO-2(7) samples over {1, . . . , 7} with π = (0.520, 0.220, 0.121, 0.068, 0.036, 0.019, 0.016), giving
E[D] = 2.
Supervised Finetuning: Every configuration is finetuned with the same recipe, 5,000 steps over one pass
of an in-house chat corpus at context length 32,768, keeping its pretrained MTP depths.
Computational Cost: Each pretraining run takes approximately 3 days on 192 NVIDIA H800 GPUs with
a global batch size of 1,920, followed by 5 hours of SFT on the same hardware. Post-hoc drafter training
uses 24 H800 GPUs with a global batch size of 48, requiring about 22 hours for EAGLE-3, 19 hours for
DFlash, and 55 hours for DSpark.
Benchmark Evaluation: Fourteen few-shot (Brown et al., 2020) benchmarks decoded greedily, grouped
by task form into mathematics, reasoning, multi-subject multiple choice, code and closed-book question
answering, with CoT prompting (Wei et al., 2022) on MATH, GSM8K and BBH.
Speculative decoding: We adopt the same temperature setting (T = 1) for both the target and draft
models, following Bebop (Li et al., 2026b) and GLM-5.2 (GLM Team, 2026). This matters for RL training:
rejection sampling with draft temperature> 0 keeps acceptance stable as target entropy rises, whereas
greedy drafting degrades sharply at higher temperatures (Li et al., 2026b). All drafters are served with
SGLang on a single node of eight H800 GPUs and budget-matched at seven proposed positions plus one
bonus token per verification step. We report decode-only throughput and mean accepted length on ten
tasks, split by the target model’s predictive entropy into predictable and open-ended groups.

7 Conclusion
We presented ECHO, which turns multi-token prediction from an auxiliary task into part of the autore-
gressive objective. By reusing the backbone’s own final layer at every horizon, ECHO lets multi-horizon
supervision shape the parameters that generate the next token, and Dynamic Depth Sampling makes
deeper horizons affordable by training them less often. Unlike auxiliary MTP such as DeepSeek, ECHO
improves with added depth, and ECHO-1.5(3) matches the downstream gain of ECHO-3 at half the MTP
overhead.
At inference, the same shared layer makes the pretrained model its own drafter with close draft-target
alignment: it drafts beyond its trained depth where auxiliary modules saturate, and it outperforms
post-hoc drafters on open-ended, high-entropy generation. This advantage holds under a doubled token
budget, a repeated pretraining run, and supervised finetuning.
Limitations
Due to compute constraints, pretraining was repeated once and finetuning was run with a single seed.
How far the gains from added depth and from depth extrapolation extend, how DDS behaves at greater
depths, and why deeper sampling pays off only after finetuning are left to future work.

A Experiment Setup
A.1 Pretraining
Model Architecture: The backbone is a 10B-A1B Latent Mixture-of-Experts transformer (Blakeman et al.,
2025), that is 10B parameters in total with 1B activated per token, stacked as 24 layers of hidden size 1,024
with 16 attention heads. Attention is multi-head latent (Liu et al., 2024a), with 512-dimensional query
and key–value latents, and we add gated attention (Qiu et al., 2025), per-head query–key normalization
and rotary positional embeddings (Su et al., 2024). Every layer is a mixture of experts in a latent space:
the expert block projects the hidden state down to 512 dimensions, runs 256 fine-grained routed experts
(Dai et al., 2024) of intermediate size 1,024 there, and projects back, with the router reading the hidden
state rather than the latent. A sigmoid gate sends each token to eight routed experts, balanced by a
sequence-level auxiliary loss, plus one shared expert. The SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020) feed-forward width is
5,120 and the vocabulary holds 201,216 tokens.
Training Settings: We train the model on 600B tokens with a context sequence length of 4,096 and a
global batch size of 1,920. Training uses bfloat16 precision and the Muon optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024),
with AdamW retained for the embeddings, the biases, the output head and the expert router, a learning
rate of 6 × 10−4, a warmup of 1,000 steps, weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. Following Liu
et al. (2024a), we set the MTP loss scaling factor to 0.3. Pretraining runs on 24 nodes of eight H800 GPUs
each, with eight-way expert parallelism, stage-one optimizer sharding, no tensor or pipeline parallelism,
and full activation recomputation.
Computational Cost: Each pretraining run is conducted on 24 nodes with eight NVIDIA H800 80GB
GPUs per node (192 GPUs in total), taking approximately three days to complete. Supervised finetuning
uses the same hardware configuration and takes approximately five hours per run. For the post-hoc
speculative decoding baselines, we train the draft models on three nodes (24 H800 80GB GPUs), using
a global batch size of 48. EAGLE-3 (cold-start and warm-start) and DFlash use a per-GPU batch size
of 2 without gradient accumulation, whereas DSpark uses a per-GPU batch size of 1 with two gradient
accumulation steps due to GPU memory constraints. EAGLE-3 requires approximately 22 hours per run
in measured wall-clock time. Based on the recorded compute time and an estimated 1.27× overhead for
startup, checkpointing, and cluster variability, DSpark requires approximately 55 hours, while DFlash
requires approximately 19 hours.
Methods Compared: All six configurations are trained under the recipe above, so the comparison isolates
the MTP design. ECHO reuses the backbone’s last layer as the body of every MTP depth; DeepSeek’s
MTP baselines follow Liu et al. (2024a) and keep an independent module per depth, sharing only the
embedding and the output head. ECHO-1 and MTP-1 train one depth, ECHO-3 and MTP-3 train three at
every step, ECHO-1.5(3) samples depths one to three with E[D] = 1.5, and ECHO-2(7) extends dynamic
depth sampling to depths one through seven with E[D] = 2. The two cost-matched comparisons are
ECHO-1 against MTP-1 at equal depth-one cost, and ECHO-1.5(3) against MTP-3 at half the MTP cost.
Dynamic Depth Sampling: The sampled depth is D ∼ π, where π places probabilities 0.6, 0.3 and 0.1 on
depths 1, 2 and 3, so E[D] = 1.5. Each optimizer step draws one D and trains depths 1 through D, and
every micro-batch in the step shares the same D, which keeps per-step compute constant. Averaging the
loss over the D trained depths gives depth d the weight E[1{D ≥ d}/D], and these weights sum to one:
ECHO reweights depths by how often it samples them, not by attaching a per-depth loss coefficient. D is
a deterministic function of the seed and the step index, so every rank derives it without communication
and a resumed run continues the same sequence.
Supervised Finetuning: All configurations adopt an identical recipe: 5,000 steps over one pass of an
in-house chat SFT corpus at a context length of 32,768, with eight-way context parallelism. Each keeps
the MTP depths it was pretrained with, so the finetuned comparison isolates the same design difference
as the pretrained one.

WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021) and PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020); multi-subject multiple choice with

(Liu et al., 2023) and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025); closed-book question answering with PopQA
(Mallen et al., 2023), PreciseWikiQA (Bang et al., 2025) and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018). We report
unweighted averages within each group and across all fourteen benchmarks. Benchmarks such as GPQA

A.3 Speculative Decoding under Serving
Serving Stack: All serving measurements use SGLang on one node of eight H800 GPUs, with tensor
parallelism 8 and FlashAttention-3.
Drafters: All drafters are budget-matched at seven proposed positions plus one target bonus token, i.e.
eight verification slots per request per step. ECHO reuses the target’s final layer and full output head.
EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025) uses one draft layer with a reduced 32,000-entry head scattered back to target
width, in a cold-start and a warm-start variant; following Kimi Team (2026), the warm start initializes the
draft from DeepSeek’s pretrained MTP weights, and is always normalized by the baseline of its own run.
DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026) and DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) are block-parallel with five draft layers, the
former adding a low-rank Markov transition bias.
Serving Metric: We report decode-only throughput, decoded tokens over summed per-request decode
latency, which excludes prefill; metrics that include prefill confound the comparison across tasks with
differing prompt lengths. Speedup is recomputed against the baseline of the same run, and accepted
length is decoded tokens per verification step.
Tasks: Serving results use ten tasks in two groups, split by predictive entropy as in Liu et al. (2026). Pre-
dictable, following the commonly adopted SpecForge benchmarks (Li et al., 2026a): HumanEval, MBPP,
MATH-500, GSM8K, LiveCodeBench. Open-ended: SWE-bench, Arena-Hard, MT-Bench, summarization,
Alpaca. Question answering, retrieval-augmented generation, translation and AIME-25 are excluded,
all having median outputs of twenty to thirty tokens, too few verification steps for a stable decode-only
ratio. The entropy axis is the target’s own predictive entropy, measured separately.
Verification Budget: DSpark is evaluated with its confidence scheduler disabled, verifying the full draft
block at every step, as in the diagnostic baseline of Cheng et al. (2026). The scheduler is a calibrated cost
model rather than a threshold: it needs a latency curve re-profiled for each GPU and backend (Cheng
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026), and a confidence head calibrated on the serving distribution, since the
survival probabilities it multiplies must be absolute rather than merely well-ranked. That calibration
does not transfer, because the mechanism assumes acceptance varies sharply across domains in the first
place. With the head enabled but neither artifact available, per-step cost rose 5–14% and nothing was
pruned.
Depth Extrapolation: The depth sweep of Figure 1b uses a separate harness. We report the accepted
length obtained at serving depth K, τK = 1 + ∑j≤K ∏i≤j pi, for conditional acceptance rate pi at depth i.

constraint is regularization, and regularization does not surface in the quantity it constrains: next-token
