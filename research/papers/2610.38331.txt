Multi-Token Prediction Done Right
Jayden Teoh
MIT
Abstract
Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) is now a common ingredient in language-model pre-training, as it improves downstream
performance and enables faster inference through speculative decoding. We identify a flaw in standard MTP training, in which a
residual component of the MTP gradient interferes with both next-token and multi-token learning, increasingly so with scale.
Specifically, the MTP cross-entropy gradient can be decomposed exactly into a KL self-distillation term that pulls each MTP
head toward the model’s own next-token prediction (NTP) distribution and improves speculative decoding, and a residual
that has a low signal-to-noise ratio and buries the useful KL signal. We introduce the KL gradient–recovery trick: when
two distributions share a linear output head, the gradient of their KL divergence can be recovered exactly by combining their
cross-entropy gradients through straight-through gradient routing. Unlike minimizing the KL directly, this trick requires no
vocabulary-sized logit matrices and is compatible with standard fused cross-entropy kernels. This enables Soft Multi-Token
Prediction (Soft-MTP), a one-line code change to MTP that removes the noisy residual and backpropagates only the KL
self-distillation signal by reusing the existing NTP and MTP cross-entropy computations. Soft-MTP supports both DeepSeek-
and Meta-style MTP, adds no parameters, and incurs no measurable overhead. From 167M to 1.2B parameters, Soft-MTP
improves over standard MTP with lower NTP perplexity, faster speculative decoding and higher zero-shot accuracy at every
scale, lower MTP perplexity from 689M onward, and stronger math and code reasoning after fine-tuning at 1.2B. Soft-MTP
also matches, if not exceeds, direct KL divergence minimization on most metrics. Soft-MTP is, in effect, a free lunch for MTP
optimization. Our code can be found at https://github.com/JaydenTeoh/Soft-MTP.

1 Introduction
Next-token prediction (NTP) has become a general recipe for training large language models (LLMs), yielding models with
remarkable capabilities across a wide range of tasks [Radford et al., 2019]. One might argue that the NTP objective is sufficient—
any distribution over token sequences can, in theory, be factorized autoregressively and modeled by a sufficiently expressive
next-token predictor [Sutskever, 2023].
Yet, Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) [Stern et al., 2018, Qi et al., 2020, Gloeckle et al., 2024], which trains a model to predict
multiple future tokens, has now been widely adopted in modern LLM training, including in DeepSeek-V3/4 [DeepSeek-AI, 2024,
2026a], Nemotron 3 [NVIDIA, 2025], GLM-5 [GLM-5-Team, 2026], and Kimi K3 [Kimi Team, 2026], just to name a few. MTP
offers two main advantages over NTP—better model performance and more efficient inference. As a learning objective, MTP
has been shown to alleviate the myopic nature of the next-token supervision [Bachmann and Nagarajan, 2024, Gloeckle et al.,
2024, Nagarajan et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2026], leading to stronger downstream performance on long-horizon domains such as
mathematics and code. More importantly, MTP heads can also be used for speculative decoding [Leviathan et al., 2022, Chen
et al., 2023] to accelerate model inference.
Despite the wide adoption of MTP, we still understand little about how it interacts with next-token prediction and which objective
is best for training the MTP heads. During speculative decoding, a token drafted by MTP is accepted only if it agrees with
the model’s own next-token prediction. If the goal is to improve speculative decoding, shouldn’t we directly train the model’s
MTP heads to match its own NTP distribution via self-distillation? Recent work does exactly this [Teoh et al., 2025, Zhao
et al., 2026a], but naïvely minimizing an explicit KL divergence requires materializing vocabulary-sized logit tensors for both
the teacher and the student, which is costly in memory and incompatible with the standard fused cross-entropy kernels used
in modern pre-training [Hsu et al., 2025, Wijmans et al., 2025]. In practice, frontier models still train their MTP heads with
standard cross-entropy against the data.
In this paper, we show that the standard MTP cross-entropy gradient already contains this self-distillation signal. The MTP
gradient decomposes exactly into a KL term gKL, which pulls the MTP head toward the model’s own next-token distribution, and
a residual gres, which is the NTP cross-entropy gradient against the data (see Section 4). We provide evidence that this residual
in the MTP loss gradient has a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and interferes with both NTP and MTP learning, especially
as scale increases. We then introduce the KL gradient–recovery trick: when two distributions share a linear output head, the
gradient of their KL divergence can be recovered exactly from their cross-entropy gradients through straight-through gradient
routing [Bengio et al., 2013, van den Oord et al., 2017], without explicitly computing the KL or materializing any logits. This
trick enables Soft Multi-Token Prediction (Soft-MTP), which removes the residual and sends only the useful KL self-distillation
gradient through the MTP heads and the shared model trunk, with a one-line code change (see Figure 1).
From 167M to 1.2B parameters, Soft-MTP improves over standard MTP with lower NTP perplexity, faster self-speculative
decoding in every domain we test, and higher zero-shot accuracy at every scale, as well as stronger code and math reasoning after
fine-tuning. It also reaches lower MTP loss from 689M parameters onward. Soft-MTP matches or exceeds the costly explicit KL
self-distillation on most metrics, at no extra cost over standard MTP. Soft-MTP supports both DeepSeek-style [DeepSeek-AI,
2024] and Meta-style [Gloeckle et al., 2024] MTP architectures, adds no parameters, has negligible overhead, and remains
compatible with fused cross-entropy kernels and the parallelism used in frontier pre-training. Soft-MTP is, in effect, a free lunch
for MTP optimization.
2 Related Work
Multi-token prediction. Predicting several future tokens dates back to blockwise parallel decoding [Stern et al., 2018] and
ProphetNet [Qi et al., 2020]. Gloeckle et al. [2024] showed that MTP with parallel output heads improves large language models,
especially on code, and DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-AI, 2024] brought MTP to frontier-scale pre-training and introduced the
sequential MTP modules now used by many frontier models [DeepSeek-AI, 2024, Qwen Team, 2025, LongCat, 2025, GLM
Team, 2025, Ling Team, 2025, NVIDIA, 2025, Xiaomi, 2026, GLM-5-Team, 2026, DeepSeek-AI, 2026a, MiniMax, 2026, Kimi
Team, 2026]. A line of work argues that MTP helps to counter the myopia of next-token supervision [Bachmann and Nagarajan,
2024, Gloeckle et al., 2024, Nagarajan et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2026]. Recent works also go beyond MTP with richer objectives
[Hu et al., 2025, Ahn et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025b, Teoh et al., 2025, Mahajan et al., 2026, Zuhri et al., 2026].
MTP for speculative decoding. Speculative decoding drafts several tokens cheaply and verifies them in parallel using the more
expensive target model [Leviathan et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2023]. MTP heads trained during pre-training can serve as drafters for
2
speculative decoding. This is especially useful for accelerating reinforcement learning post-training, where generating rollouts is
often the main bottleneck [Xiaomi, 2026, Li et al., 2026a, Iso et al., 2026]. Recent work trains drafters to match the verifier more
directly, by minimizing the TV distance [Samarin et al., 2026, Li et al., 2026a], and Nemotron 3 Ultra [NVIDIA, 2026] introduces
an MTP Boosting post-training phase that aligns the MTP heads with the NTP distribution via a temperature-scaled forward KL
objective. Soft-MTP pursues the same goal during pre-training, at no extra cost over the standard MTP objective.
Self-distillation for MTP. Recent works train MTP heads to match the NTP distribution. They either compute the KL
explicitly [Teoh et al., 2025], which requires materializing the teacher’s and the student’s distributions over the full vocabulary, or
approximate it with top-k logits [Zhao et al., 2026a] or argmax predictions [Kirchenbauer et al., 2026]. Chang and gpt-5.5-xhigh
[2026] shows a cool trick to reduce the cost of full-vocabulary distillation by caching the teacher’s contribution to the KL gradient.
However, their method still requires materializing the teacher’s full-vocabulary distribution, and it requires the student’s output
head to be frozen. We show that standard MTP already contains the self-distillation gradient, and that our KL gradient–recovery
trick recovers it exactly from the existing cross-entropy gradients. Soft-MTP therefore incurs no measurable overhead beyond
that of standard MTP and remains compatible with fused cross-entropy kernels [Hsu et al., 2025, Wijmans et al., 2025].

5 Soft Multi-Token Prediction
The earlier gradient decomposition leads to the question: how can we retain the self-distillation component gKL by removing the
residual component gres from the hard MTP gradient propagated through the shared model trunk?

A naïve implementation would explicitly construct the aligned NTP teacher distribution q(k) t and k-th MTP distribution p(k) t ,
and minimize DKL
 
sg(q(k) t ) ∥ p(k) t
 
directly in the logit space. However, given that the vocabulary size V is large in modern
language models, storing large T × V logit matrices during every MTP prediction is costly in memory usage. It is also
incompatible with standard fused cross-entropy kernels, which are designed to avoid materializing the entire logit matrix in
memory, and are commonly used in language model pre-training. A custom fused KL kernel could avoid materializing the logits,
but it would have to recompute the teacher logits that the NTP loss already computes, unless the NTP cross-entropy and all K
MTP KL terms were fused into a single custom kernel.
We instead show that the same representation-space KL gradient can be recovered exactly from the cross-entropy gradients
already computed during standard MTP training, with no measurable overhead. We term this novel procedure of recovering a KL
gradient from the difference of two cross-entropy gradients under a shared linear output head as the KL gradient–recovery trick.
We also show in Section 6.3 that recovering the KL gradient using this trick matches, if not exceeds, direct KL minimization on
most metrics.

5.2 Soft-MTP
Our method, Soft Multi-Token Prediction (Soft-MTP), uses the KL gradient–recovery trick to optimize the parameters of the
MTP heads and the shared model trunk. There are two main distinctions of Soft-MTP compared to prior self-distillation MTP
approaches.
First, the KL gradient–recovery trick operates directly in the NTP hidden-state space (see Equation 4), and avoids the need to
materialize any additional logit matrices or explicitly compute KL divergence. As a result, Soft-MTP introduces negligible
computational and memory overhead over standard MTP.
Second, Soft-MTP still sends cross-entropy gradients from the MTP loss into the shared linear output head Wθ , but the parameters
of the upstream modules (i.e., MTP heads and shared model trunk) receive the self-distillation gradient gKL instead. We explain
why this is better than direct KL minimization in Section 6.3. Table 1 presents the anatomy of the gradient routing across
different MTP objectives.

5.3 A bias–variance view of the residual
Equation 2 can also be interpreted from a bias–variance decomposition perspective. Of its two components, only gres depends on
the sampled target token xt+k+1; gKL is determined entirely by the model’s own NTP and MTP predictions. Let π(k) t denote the
true distribution of the next token xt+k+1 given the prefix x≤t+k, which is exactly what the position-aligned NTP distribution
q(k) t tries to predict. Since the one-hot target is a sample xt+k+1 ∼ π(k) t , the residual splits into two parts:

where the sampling noise has zero mean because E[xt+k+1] = π(k) t . The hard MTP gradient is unbiased but it includes the gres
component and incurs the full one-hot sampling variance. By removing gres from the MTP updates, Soft-MTP removes this
variance at the cost of a bias: without the NTP-error correction term, gKL is biased toward the NTP distribution and not the data
distribution π(k) t . These two terms behave very differently as models scale. The bias term is the NTP teacher’s own prediction
error, which keeps shrinking as the base model scales and/or trains longer. The variance term is the irreducible noise of sampling
a one-hot target, which no amount of scale or training removes. Therefore, the trade that Soft-MTP makes becomes increasingly
favorable at scale. We observe this empirically in Section 6.1, where the residual has a much lower signal-to-noise ratio than the
KL component, and the gap widens with model scale. Furthermore, since MTP is largely used to accelerate inference, biasing
the MTP heads towards matching the NTP distribution is actually desirable for improving speculative decoding acceptance2.
Unlike KL-MTP, Soft-MTP still trains the shared output head Wθ with cross-entropy, so the head is fitted to the data rather than
biased toward its own next-token predictions. We explain why this matters in Section 6.3.

6 Experiments
We now turn to an empirical validation of Soft-MTP. Our study is organized around three main questions:
1. How do the self-distillation component gKL and residual component gres of the MTP cross-entropy gradient affect
next-token and multi-token learning?
In Section 6.1, we find that gres has a substantially lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) than gKL, yet accounts for a large
fraction of the MTP gradient norm. Moreover, this residual becomes increasingly noisy with model scale, obscuring the
useful learning signal in the MTP cross-entropy gradient. We further find that the residual component is anti-aligned with
the NTP update direction throughout training, suggesting that the residual not only adds noise to MTP learning but also
interferes with NTP optimization. Soft-MTP reaches a lower NTP loss than standard MTP, and the gap widens with scale.
From 689M parameters onward, it also reaches a lower MTP loss.
2. Does isolating the self-distillation gradient in Soft-MTP improve over standard MTP?
In Section 6.2, we empirically validate the analysis above by comparing Soft-MTP against standard MTP. Across model
scales, Soft-MTP yields better perplexity, downstream accuracy, speculative-decoding acceptance rates, and math and code
fine-tuning performance. These results support our analyses in Question 1—removing the noisy residual produces a cleaner
and more compatible learning signal.
3. How does Soft-MTP compare with directly optimizing a KL self-distillation objective?
In Section 6.3, we find little benefit from explicitly computing the KL divergence or propagating its gradients through the
LM head: retaining hard cross-entropy supervision at the LM head while propagating the recovered KL gradients only
through the MTP heads and shared model backbone is sufficient to match or exceed the gains of direct self-distillation.
Moreover, Soft-MTP avoids the computational overhead of computing KL divergence and preserves compatibility with
fused linear cross-entropy kernels. Overall, this suggests that Soft-MTP is more efficient.
Experimental setup. We pre-trained LLaMA-style models [Touvron et al., 2023] of base-model sizes ranging from 167M to
1.2B parameters using NTP and MTP objectives. For MTP, we used the widely adopted DeepSeek-style sequential architecture
[DeepSeek-AI, 2024] and added three MTP heads to predict K = 3 additional future tokens, following common practice
[Gloeckle et al., 2024, Xiaomi, 2026, MiniMax, 2026, GLM-5-Team, 2026]. To study the effect of standard MTP and Soft-MTP
consistently across model sizes, we kept each MTP head at a constant 3.2% of the trunk’s non-embedding parameters and fixed
the MTP loss coefficient at λMTP = 1.0. We trained all models on the FineWeb-Edu dataset [Penedo et al., 2024] for 60,000
steps with a batch size of 0.5M tokens and a sequence length of 2048, for a total of approximately 31.5B training tokens, which
is ∼ 1.3× Chinchilla-optimal [Hoffmann et al., 2022] for our largest model size. To be of modern relevance, we trained our
models using the Muon optimizer [Jordan et al., 2024b] under the update-RMS rescaling of Liu et al. [2025a]. Our training
hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix D. For completeness, we also showed that Soft-MTP improves over standard MTP
using Meta-style MTP heads in Appendix C.

Gradient signal-to-noise. We first measure the signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) of the different gradient components w.r.t. the
model parameters θ: gθ hard (hard MTP gradient), gθ KL (KL self-distillation gradient), gθ res (residual MTP gradient), and gθ NTP (NTP
cross-entropy gradient). For a gradient component gθ c (ξ) computed on a data batch ξ, we define its signal as the squared norm of
its expected direction, Sc = ∥Eξ [gθ c ]∥2, and its noise as the total variance around this direction, Nc = Eξ ∥gθ c ∥2 − Sc = tr(Σc).
We report SNRc = Sc/Nc as the signal-to-noise ratio. This quantity is closely related to the inverse of the gradient noise scale of
McCandlish et al. [2018]; with a fixed batch size, the two differ only by a constant factor. We estimate the SNR using M = 256
independent batches, each containing 4 sequences of length 2048. The naïve signal estimate is biased upward by tr(Σ)/M , so
we report the unbiased estimator following McCandlish et al. [2018, Appendix A.1].
Figures 3a and 3b compare the SNR of the two gradient components w.r.t. the shared trunk and MTP head parameters across
model scales. Throughout training, the residual carries roughly 4× lower SNR than the KL component. The residual
becomes increasingly noisy, and the gap widens as the scale increases, from 4.1× at 167M to 4.6× at 1.2B (note the log-scale
axis). This result corroborates the bias–variance tradeoff discussed in Section 5.3: the residual always contains irreducible
noise from sampling the one-hot target, while its signal, i.e., the NTP error, shrinks with scale, so its SNR falls as models
grow. Removing the residual substantially increases gradient SNR—the KL component has much higher SNR than the original
hard-MTP gradient. Yet despite its low SNR, the residual accounts for ∼80% of the MTP gradient reaching the shared trunk
(see Figure 3c). Thus, although the standard MTP contains the high SNR KL signal implicitly, the residual obscures it under
high-variance updates, which is why the overall hard-MTP gradient has lower SNR (see Figure 3b). We detail how we measure
the SNR and gradient share of the KL and residual components in Appendix E.

Interaction with NTP and MTP losses. Earlier, we showed the residual has low SNR. Here, we aim to show that the residual
is not only noisy, but also interferes with NTP and MTP learning. In Figure 4a, we take a unit step on the shared trunk’s hidden
state along each component’s gradient (backpropagated from the MTP heads) and re-evaluate the NTP loss through the frozen
output head. This is a proxy for what each component ‘asks’ the trunk output to become. A step along the KL component
consistently decreases NTP loss, indicating that the self-distillation signal in MTP reinforces the NTP objective. In contrast,
a step along the residual component in MTP consistently increases NTP loss. This behavior persists throughout training and
is consistent across model scales. Note that this analysis serves only as a characterization of the components’ behavior at the
trunk–MTP head interface; the realized update to the trunk’s hidden states during training also depends on the optimizer used.
We empirically corroborate this residual interference in Figure 4b: removing the residual with Soft-MTP lowers the NTP loss,
especially as scale increases. Furthermore, from 689M parameters onward, Soft-MTP also reaches a lower MTP loss than
standard MTP (see Figure 4c).
Summary: The residual is much noisier (lower SNR) than the KL component, yet accounts for roughly 80% of the MTP
gradient. Removing the residual gradient improves NTP and MTP learning, especially as scale increases.

6.3 Soft-MTP is more efficient than KL-MTP
KL-MTP is inefficient in compute and memory. Table 5 compares the training cost of each objective at 1.2B. Because the
KL gradient–recovery trick reuses the cross-entropy gradients that standard MTP already computes, Soft-MTP has the same
matmul FLOPs, MFU and fused-CE peak memory as standard MTP, and its throughput is within 0.07%. KL-MTP is the most
expensive on every axis. KL-MTP materializes both the teacher and student distributions over the vocabulary, which adds 1.5%
FLOPs, lowers throughput by 5.6% relative to standard MTP, and raises peak memory by 21 GB. It also gains little benefit from
standard fused cross-entropy kernels because the KL objective needs dense distributions to be materialized.

used to update the output head, and we next explain why training it with cross-entropy, as Soft-MTP does, matters.

Why keep cross-entropy at the output head? Note that the output head Wθ is shared in MTP; the same matrix maps both
the NTP and the MTP hidden states to logits. As such, any gradient from the MTP loss also shapes the NTP readout. Under
Soft-MTP, this gradient is the MTP cross-entropy gradient which, like the NTP loss, fits the output head to the data distribution.
Under KL-MTP, it is the self-distillation gradient, which fits the head to the model’s own NTP predictions, and those predictions
are themselves also computed by Wθ . This creates a shortcut because the KL can be reduced not only by improving the MTP
hidden states, but also by making the shared readout less sensitive to the directions in which the MTP and NTP hidden states differ.

Cross-entropy has no such shortcut because its target does not depend on
Wθ . Figure 6 shows the consequence for the readout’s geometry, measured
by the effective rank [Roy and Vetterli, 2007] of the logit map Wγ , the
unembedding combined with the final RMSNorm gain γ (we combine
the two because the logits depend only on their product). Standard MTP
collapses the readout to about a quarter of the NTP baseline (131 vs. 496)
and KL-MTP to about two thirds (321), while Soft-MTP stays closest (437).
The gradient routing differences between the objectives (see Table 1) allow
us to pull the two effects apart. Soft-MTP and KL-MTP both send the KL
gradient through the MTP heads and trunk and differ only at the output
head, so the gap between them comes from keeping cross-entropy at the
head. Soft-MTP and standard MTP both use cross-entropy at the head
and differ only in the trunk, so the much larger gap comes from removing
the residual. Soft-MTP combines the best of both worlds in terms of
gradient routing, and it is also the objective with the strongest perplexity
and downstream accuracy results.
Summary: Soft-MTP recovers the KL self-distillation gradient at the same cost as standard MTP, whereas KL-MTP is less
efficient in throughput and memory. Soft-MTP nonetheless matches or exceeds KL-MTP on most metrics.
7 Discussion: Practical Relevance at Frontier Scale
MTP has been widely adopted in modern LLM training. Frontier model pre-training leverages many parallelism schemes due to
tight memory budgets. Here we discuss the compatibility and relevance of Soft-MTP in frontier model pre-training.
Compatibility with parallelism. The KL gradient–recovery trick needs only two things where it is applied: the NTP hidden
state h(0) t+k and the MTP hidden state h(k) t on the same device, and a backward path from LNTP to h(k) t . On its own, it adds
no communication and no synchronization. Parallelism that shards parameters, optimizer state or experts therefore leaves it
untouched, e.g., data parallelism and ZeRO [Rajbhandari et al., 2020], and expert parallelism [Lepikhin et al., 2021] as used by
DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-AI, 2024] and Kimi K3 [Kimi Team, 2026]. Pipeline parallelism [Huang et al., 2019] only requires the
MTP modules to sit on the same stage as the output head, which DeepSeek-V3 already does by design so that its MTP module
can share the output head. The only axis that needs extra communication is sequence sharding, because our trick reads hidden
states k tokens ahead. This includes context parallelism, such as ring attention [Liu et al., 2024], and sequence parallelism
[Korthikanti et al., 2022]. Even then, the cost is a backward-pass exchange of at most K hidden-state gradients at each shard
boundary, independent of sequence length.
KL gradient–recovery trick is more compatible than explicit self-distillation. An explicit KL needs the teacher’s and the
student’s vocabulary-sized distributions at once, which standard fused cross-entropy kernels cannot compute without materializing
the logits. This is especially costly for models trained without tensor parallelism, such as DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-AI, 2024],
which therefore cannot shard these tensors along the vocabulary. A custom fused KL kernel can avoid this materialization, but
must recompute the teacher logits that the NTP loss already computes, unless the NTP cross-entropy and all MTP KL terms are
fused into a single kernel. The KL gradient–recovery trick instead carries the KL gradient through the hidden state rather than
the logits, so every head keeps its standard, kernel-fused cross-entropy [Hsu et al., 2025, Wijmans et al., 2025], and Soft-MTP
adds no vocabulary-sized tensors beyond those standard MTP already uses. Soft-MTP also matches, if not exceeds, direct KL
minimization on most metrics as shown in Section 6.3.
12
Future of MTP and extensions to the KL gradient–recovery trick. MTP has spread rapidly since DeepSeek-V3 adopted
it in 2024 [DeepSeek-AI, 2024], but its longevity in pre-training is uncertain. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [DeepSeek-AI, 2026b]
recently dropped MTP in favor of DSpark [Cheng et al., 2026], a speculative decoder trained after pre-training. Post-trained
drafters such as Medusa [Cai et al., 2024], EAGLE [Li et al., 2024a,b, 2026b], DFlash [Chen et al., 2026] and DSpark [Cheng
et al., 2026] are convenient, but it remains open how well they work when the base model was never trained to form predictive
representations of future tokens, e.g., via MTP. Will MTP be retained for the superior downstream accuracy it provides over
NTP-only pre-training [Gloeckle et al., 2024, DeepSeek-AI, 2024, MiniMax, 2026, NVIDIA, 2025], at the cost of minimal
additional FLOPs? MTP has also proven useful in small-scale pre-training, e.g., in Modded-NanoGPT [Jordan et al., 2024a], and
in data-constrained settings, where it improves sample efficiency, e.g., in NanoGPT Slowrun [Vegesna et al., 2026]. Whatever
the future of MTP, the KL gradient–recovery trick applies more broadly—whenever a teacher and a student share a linear output
head, the gradient of the forward KL can be recovered from their cross-entropy gradients. Many post-training methods compute
such a KL, for example MTP Boosting [NVIDIA, 2026], on-policy self-distillation (OPSD; Zhao et al., 2026b), and score
centering for off-policy RL [Marek and Ryabinin, 2026]. Such use cases are expensive due to the KL computation overhead,
which may necessitate top-k logits approximation. Our KL gradient–recovery trick avoids that cost, with the caveat that only the
MTP heads and shared trunk receive the exact KL gradient, whereas the shared output head Wθ is updated with cross-entropy.
This is also suitable for methods that already freeze the output head under the KL loss, such as Next-Latent Prediction [Teoh
et al., 2025], which only needs the KL gradient with respect to the hidden states.
8 Conclusion
The standard MTP cross-entropy gradient can be decomposed into a KL self-distillation term, which pulls the MTP heads
toward the model’s own next-token distribution, and a residual, which is the next-token cross-entropy gradient against the data.
The residual carries most of the MTP gradient, yet it has a low signal-to-noise ratio, works against next-token learning, and
becomes noisier with scale. Our KL gradient–recovery trick removes this residual and recovers the KL term exactly from the
cross-entropy gradients that standard MTP already computes. This yields Soft-MTP, a one-line change to standard MTP that
adds no parameters, has negligible overhead, and remains compatible with fused cross-entropy kernels. Soft-MTP improves
over standard MTP in NTP and MTP loss, self-speculative decoding, zero-shot accuracy, and in math and code reasoning after
fine-tuning, all at no extra cost.

A Full Soft-MTP PyTorch Implementation
Algorithm 1 Full Soft-MTP implementation in PyTorch syntax, for DeepSeek-style sequential MTP. The scale sk rescales each
routed gradient to match the corresponding head’s own cross-entropy gradient (Section B.5); the highlighted lines are the only
changes from standard MTP.
# x, y : [B, T] input tokens and pre-shifted labels; y[:, t] is the
# target of position t.
# K : number of MTP heads.
# lambda_mtp : auxiliary MTP weight.
# ce : cross-entropy, mean-reduced over the targets it is given.
#
# MTP head k at position t and the NTP head at position t+k predict the
# SAME token y[:, t+k]; that shared label is what cancels the label term
# and leaves the KL gradient. Each MTP head has one fewer target than the
# head before it: head k has T-k targets, y[:, k:].
h_prev = shared_trunk(x)
h0 = norm[0](h_prev) # NTP hidden states are directly from trunk, [B, T, d]
T = y.size(1)
loss_mtp = 0
for k in range(1, K+1):
yk = y[:, k:] # [B, T-k] k-th MTP head targets
h_prev = mtp_head[k-1](h_prev[:, :T-k], x[:, k:]) # DeepSeek's sequential MTP
hk = norm[k](h_prev)
# hard CE: hk gets lambda_mtp/(K(T-k)) * CE gradient
loss_mtp += ce(lm_head, hk, yk)
# straight-through gradient routing
# s_k undoes the NTP CE's 1/T mean and re-applies k-th MTP head's own
# 1/(T-k) mean and the 1/K head average, so the routed gradient
# lands at exactly the scale the MTP head's own CE gradient uses.
s_k = lambda_mtp * T / (K * (T - k))
# h0 is numerically unchanged; only its backward changes
h0[:, k:] = h0[:, k:] + s_k * (hk.detach() - hk)
# NTP loss and the gradients reaching the trunk through h0 are unchanged
loss_ntp = ce(lm_head, h0, y)
# backward: hk[t] gets -s_k * (NTP CE grad at h0[t+k]); its one-hot part
# cancels the one in hk's own CE grad, leaving the KL gradient
# lambda_mtp / (K(T-k)) * W^T (p_k[t] - p_0[t+k])
(loss_ntp + lambda_mtp/K * loss_mtp).backward()

E.1 Gradient signal-to-noise ratio
Let {gi}M i=1 be the per-minibatch mean gradients of one component, with mean µ and covariance Σ. Writing ¯g = 1 M P i gi,
signal = ∥¯g∥2, total = 1 M X
i
∥gi∥2, noise = total − signal,
and SNR = signal/noise. Its reciprocal is the gradient noise scale of McCandlish et al. [2018], measured in units of minibatches.
The raw ratio is biased: the measured signal carries a tr Σ/M sampling floor and the measured noise is low by a factor
(M − 1)/M. We report the debiased value throughout.
E.2 Share of the MTP gradient
Because ghard = gKL + gres holds exactly and per batch, each component’s contribution to the applied gradient is its projection
onto it:
sharec = ⟨ghard, gc⟩ ∥ghard∥2 , shareKL + shareres = 1 exactly.
Expanding ∥ghard∥2 = ∥gKL∥2 + ∥gres∥2 + 2⟨gKL, gres⟩ expresses both shares in terms of three scalars, ∥gKL∥2, ∥gres∥2
and ⟨gKL, gres⟩, which we pool over batches before taking the ratio. Because the shares sum to one by construction, they
decompose ghard rather than merely comparing magnitudes. A share can be negative when the two components point in opposite
directions.
E.3 Proxy effect of each component on the NTP loss
To ask what a unit step along one gradient component would do to the next-token loss, we take that step in hidden space and
re-evaluate. With H the (detached) trunk hidden state, gc the component’s gradient backpropagated from the MTP hidden state
through the MTP head to H, and LNTP the NTP cross-entropy,
∆L(c) NTP = LNTP(RMSNormNTP(H − gc)) − LNTP(RMSNormNTP(H)) , positive = harm,
evaluated separately for c ∈ {KL, residual}; positive values mean the step increases the NTP loss. Note that this proxy only
probes what one component’s direction would do to the NTP loss at this state, not what training actually does to the final
model.
