TRAINING SPECIALIST LLMS ON YOUR PHONE: FORWARD-ONLY
OPTIMIZATION VIA BLOCK-SPECTRAL COORDINATES
Liyan Tan * 1 Yequan Zhao * 1 Ziyue Liu 2 Yupeng Su 2 Ruijie Zhang 2 Zheng Zhang 1
ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) on personal devices keep user data local, but limited memory restricts their
general capability. Since most user-specific tasks do not require the breadth of frontier models, a practical solution
is to adapt a shared on-device model into lightweight specialists. However, backpropagation is prohibitive in
memory, and users rarely provide labeled data. We present MobileSpec, a system that trains specialists with
forward passes alone and learns from automatically checked task outcomes, as in reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR). Because forward-only updates are built from random perturbations, training is efficient
only when these perturbations resemble the weight changes of RLVR, which largely preserve the singular values
of pretrained weights and rotate their singular vectors. The core algorithm of MobileSpec, FuRA-S, produces
such perturbations while training only the block-spectral coordinates of pretrained weights, namely the singular
values of their column blocks. As we show theoretically and empirically, rescaling these blocks independently
shifts their relative contributions to each weight matrix and thereby rotates its singular vectors. Since this update
can be rewritten as a transformation of the layer input, FuRA-S also adapts INT4 models without modifying their
weights. With specialists of only 1–5 MB that update 0.015–0.05% of the parameters, MobileSpec raises the
success rate of a phone-use agent from 6–23% up to 89–100% and trains a personalized 8B model on an iPhone
16 Pro overnight. On RLVR benchmarks, FuRA-S is competitive with full-parameter evolution strategies (ES) and
outperforms ES over LoRA adapters and quantized ES on INT4 models.
1 INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed
as autonomous agents on personal devices, from desktop
environments (Xie et al., 2024) to mobile interfaces (Wang
et al., 2024). With the rapid progress of small language
models (Liu et al., 2024) and the dedicated inference accel-
erators (NPUs) in recent smartphones, running such agents
entirely on the device has become practical. These agents
keep user data local and work offline.
However, the limited memory of mobile devices restricts
on-device models to a few billion parameters, leaving their
general capability well below that of frontier models. In
practice, users rarely need such breadth, since the tasks
they perform on the device are highly user-specific. This
motivates a deployment paradigm in which a shared base
model, referred to as the generalist, is adapted locally into
lightweight specialists. Each specialist is a small set of
parameters trained for one of these tasks.

ference (Malladi et al., 2023) and is poorly supported by
the quantized dataflow of on-device engines (Zhao et al.,
2025). Forward-only methods, including zeroth-order (ZO)
optimization (Malladi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025) and
evolution strategies (ES; Qiu et al., 2026; Sarkar et al., 2026),
avoid these costs by estimating updates from the losses or
rewards of randomly perturbed models. Yet they still per-
turb a large number of model parameters and maintain a
full-precision copy of the weights, which exceeds the mem-
ory available to an app (Figure 1) and makes every specialist
as large as the model itself. This raises a natural question:
can a phone still train its own specialists from the signals
that arise during use, with forward passes alone?
In this work, we investigate this question and present Mo-
bileSpec, an on-device training system that realizes the
generalist–specialist paradigm (Figure 2). Its specialists
share one INT4 generalist and differ only in files of 1–5 MB,
so switching tasks leaves the generalist and its kernels un-
changed. When the phone is idle, MobileSpec trains each
specialist with forward passes, measuring the fitness of the
perturbed models by automatic checks of task outcomes or
by the likelihood of the user’s records. For long-horizon
phone-use tasks, we further design a step-wise fitness func-
tion that credits each action by comparing it with the actions
that other perturbed models take on the same screen.
How efficiently MobileSpec learns depends on which pa-
rameters its specialists change. Parameter-efficient fine-
tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) are
a natural choice, but they were designed for backpropaga-
tion. Backpropagation computes the exact gradient for any
parameterization, whereas forward-only optimization esti-
mates the update as a weighted sum of random perturbations.
Learning is therefore efficient only when these perturbations
resemble the weight changes that RLVR makes. Recent
work characterizes these changes as spectral inheritance:
RLVR largely preserves the singular values of pretrained
weights (Zhu et al., 2026a;b) and adapts the model mainly
by rotating their singular vectors (Zhu et al., 2026a). The
generic low-rank perturbations of LoRA are not designed to
produce such rotations. The parameters that existing meth-
ods train are thus either too large to store for every task or
not aligned with the weight changes of RLVR.
At the core of MobileSpec is FuRA-S (Full-Rank
Adaptation–Spectra), an extremely parameter-efficient
method for forward-only training. FuRA-S trains only the
block-spectral coordinates of pretrained weights, namely
the singular values of their column blocks under an un-
truncated singular value decomposition (SVD). Yet its ES
perturbations change the weights in the way RLVR does.
Theoretically, FuRA-S achieves this because rescaling the
singular values of each block independently shifts the rela-
tive contributions of the blocks to the assembled matrix and
thereby rotates its singular vectors rather than changing its
singular values. Empirically, what FuRA-S learns comes
almost entirely from these rotations: the resulting singular
vectors combined with the pretrained singular values retain
over 95% of the accuracy gain. The untruncated factoriza-
tion also lets FuRA-S adapt the INT4 generalist without
modifying its weights, since the update can be rewritten
exactly as a transformation of the layer input.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We design MobileSpec, an on-device training system
that realizes the generalist–specialist paradigm. Its spe-
cialists of 1–5 MB share one INT4 generalist, and its
step-wise fitness function credits the individual actions
of long-horizon phone-use tasks.
• We propose FuRA-S, the core algorithm of Mobile-
Spec, which trains only the block-spectral coordinates
of pretrained weights, about 0.03% of the parameters
of Qwen2.5-1.5B, and adapts released INT4 models
without modifying their weights.
• We show through a first-order analysis that FuRA-S
changes pretrained weights in the way RLVR does,
rotating their singular vectors while largely preserving
their singular values, and confirm empirically that the
weights it trains exhibit spectral inheritance.
• We conduct experiments on phone-use, personalization,
reward-based, supervised, and agentic tasks. Mobile-
Spec raises the success rate of a phone-use agent from
6–23% up to 89–100%. On RLVR benchmarks, FuRA-
S is competitive with full-parameter ES, consistently
outperforms ES over LoRA adapters (LoRA-ES), and
surpasses quantized evolution strategies (QES) by up
to 16.25 points.

3 THE MOBILESPEC SYSTEM
MobileSpec realizes the generalist–specialist paradigm by
keeping one INT4 generalist on the device and training a
specialist for each of the user’s tasks with forward passes
alone while the phone is idle (Figure 2). Its training algo-
rithm, FuRA-S, changes only the block singular values of
the generalist and runs directly on its INT4 weights.
3.1 Workflow
The generalist comprises the INT4 weights and frozen block
factors that are computed from them once on a server and
distributed with the model (Section 3.3). Each specialist is a
1–5 MB file of relative changes to the block singular values.
Task and fitness. Each task needs a fitness function that
the device can compute without labels. For a task that ends
in a verifiable state, such as replying to a post, the perturbed
models attempt it in sandboxed apps, and a checker inspects
the final state of the app and returns a reward. For a task
defined by the user’s own records, such as tagging movies,
the fitness of a perturbed model is the likelihood that it
assigns to these records.
Training. When the phone is idle, for example overnight,
MobileSpec trains the specialist with FuRA-S (Section 3.2).
Each iteration evaluates a population of perturbed models,
called members, and moves the specialist toward the mem-
bers with higher fitness, which for long-horizon tasks is the

step-wise fitness of Section 3.4. These forward evaluations
stream the decoder layers from flash, so the device never
holds the entire model in memory.
Selection. MobileSpec periodically evaluates the special-
ist on held-out instances of the task, such as the user’s later
records or new episodes. It retains the best-scoring check-
point and stops training once the score no longer improves.
Serving. When the user runs a task, the device loads its
specialist and applies it as a transformation of the input
before each INT4 multiplication (Section 3.3). Switching to
another task therefore replaces only the specialist, while the
generalist and its kernels stay unchanged.
3.2 Training specialists with FuRA-S
FuRA-S trains each specialist by ES over the block singular
values of the generalist (Figure 3a). They make up about
0.03% of the parameters of Qwen2.5-1.5B and form the
search space, which is the set of parameters that ES perturbs.
Although only these values change, their perturbations rotate
the singular vectors of each weight matrix while barely
changing its singular values, as RLVR does. Section 4 shows
that this property lets ES find improving perturbations for
longer and learn faster than in other search spaces.
Block-wise SVD reparameterization. Following FuRA
(Zhao et al., 2026), we split W ∈ Rm×n into K column
blocks of width b, W = [ W1 | · · · | WK ], and factor each
block by its untruncated SVD:
Wk = Lk diag(sk)R⊤ k , (1)
where Lk ∈ Rm×b has orthonormal columns, Rk ∈ Rb×b
is orthogonal, and sk ∈ Rb holds the block singular values.
Because every block keeps all of its singular values, the fac-
torization reconstructs W exactly. The product Lk diag(sk)
has the same size as Wk and takes its place, so the only
overhead in storage and computation comes from the K
factors Rk of size b × b: Kb2 = nb values per matrix, or a
fraction b/m of W. Partitioning the columns as in FuRA
(Appendix C) keeps this overhead at about 3%.
Update rule. ES updates the parameters from the fitness
of perturbed models instead of gradients. At iteration t, it
evaluates N members θt +µzj with zj ∼ N (0, Id), obtains
their fitness Fj , such as a task reward or a negative loss,
from forward passes, and updates θt+1 = θt + α N P j Fj zj ,
where the step size α absorbs 1/µ.

are sampled and weighted. FuRA-S freezes Lk and Rk and

3.4 Step-wise fitness for long-horizon tasks
In long-horizon tasks such as operating a phone app, one
wrong action can decide the outcome of an episode, yet the
agent receives only a single reward at its end. In Mobile-
World (Kong et al., 2026), for example, an agent asked to
reply to a Mastodon post often searches for the text of the
post, which the server does not index, rather than for the
account of its author, and then scrolls until it reaches the
step limit. When ES scores each member by the rewards
of its own episodes, a better action receives credit only if a
perturbation replaces the wrong action with it and thereby
alters the outcome of the member’s few episodes, in which
the randomness of all other steps masks its effect.
The step-wise fitness instead scores each action against the
whole population. Following GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025),
each step t of an episode with H steps receives the episode
reward discounted by γH−1−t, with γ ∈ (0, 1), as its re-
turn. Steps with nearly identical screenshots form an anchor
group g, in which the credit of step t is the advantage of its
action, ˆQ(gt, at) − ˆV (gt). Here ˆV (g) is the mean return of
the steps in g, and ˆQ(g, a) is that of the steps taking action
a. Unlike GiGPO, we pool the groups over all members and
sum the credits of each member’s steps into its fitness,

episodes fail for other reasons. The fitness is computed
entirely from the screenshots, actions, and rewards that
the episodes already record, adding no forward passes or
environment calls.
4 ANALYZING FURA-S THROUGH
SPECTRAL INHERITANCE
This section explains why the search space of FuRA-S suits
ES. In existing search spaces, including LoRA adapters and
all parameters, ES soon stops finding perturbations that im-
prove the model (Section 4.1). The reason lies in how RLVR
changes pretrained weights: the trained weights exhibit
spectral inheritance (Zhu et al., 2026a), and perturbations
that respect it improve the model more often (Section 4.2).
FuRA-S follows this structure with very few parameters,
since rescaling the block singular values independently ro-
tates the singular vectors of each weight matrix while barely
changing its singular values (Section 4.3). The weights it
trains also exhibit spectral inheritance (Section 4.4).
4.1 ES in existing search spaces
The search space determines both the size of a specialist
and how quickly it improves. LoRA is the natural choice,

4.2 ES under spectral inheritance
The convergence rate of ES depends on the fraction of per-
turbations that improve on the unperturbed model. Once
this fraction approaches zero, the fitness-weighted update
carries little signal and learning slows. An effective search
space must therefore keep this fraction positive.
Spectral inheritance, a property of the weight changes that
RLVR makes (Zhu et al., 2026a), points to such a search
space. Let W = UΣV⊤ be the SVD of a pretrained
weight matrix W ∈ Rm×n, where Σ = diag(σ1, . . . , σq )
holds the spectrum and q = min(m, n). The left and right
singular vectors, the orthonormal columns of U ∈ Rm×q
and V ∈ Rn×q , form the output and input singular frames.
The matrices U′ΣV′⊤ with orthonormal U′ and V′ differ
from W only in their frames and form its fixed-spectrum
family F (W). ISO (Zhu et al., 2026a) finds that RLVR
barely moves the spectra of pretrained weights, whereas it
rotates both their output and their input frames. Combining
the trained frames with the pretrained spectrum retains the
gain of RLVR, and optimizing only the frames at a fixed

Rotating the frames through the SVD of each full weight
matrix, however, is impractical on a phone. A specialist
that changes both U and V would be larger than the model
itself, and for INT4 weights these dense factors would also
need high-precision storage. Smaller alternatives fall short
as well: low-rank adapters initialized from a truncated SVD
suffer from spectral collapse in RLVR (Yin et al., 2026), and
perturbing only the global singular values, as in SVF (Sun
et al., 2022), cannot rotate the frames. The block singular
values that FuRA-S trains avoid these problems, and Sec-
tion 4.3 shows that perturbing them still rotates the frames.
4.3 How block rescaling follows spectral inheritance
We now show that FuRA-S follows spectral inheritance: its
perturbations rotate the frames of W while keeping W′
near F (W). As in ISO (Zhu et al., 2026a), we quantify how
much an update ∆W = W′ − W changes the spectrum
with two metrics (Appendix A). The spectral residual ρΣ is
the distance from W′ to F (W) relative to the update size,
ranging from 0 for a pure rotation to 1 for a pure rescaling
of singular values. The spectrum-changing energy κ is
the fraction of the update energy that changes the singular
values to first order, normalized to one for a random dense
update. We measure frame rotation by the principal angles
between the leading singular subspaces of W and W′.
Frame rotation from block rescaling. By Eq. (4), rescal-
ing each sk by 1 + δk gives W′ = W(I + E) with
E = R diag(δ)R⊤. In the singular basis of W, the update
decomposes as U⊤∆WV = ΣG with G = V⊤EV: the
diagonal of G changes the spectrum, and its off-diagonal en-
tries rotate both frames (Proposition 1, Appendix A). Rescal-
ing the singular values of each block independently shifts the
relative contributions of the blocks to the assembled matrix,
producing off-diagonal entries in G and thereby rotating
U and V. With a single block, the block SVD is the SVD
of W, so R = V, G is diagonal, and the same rescaling
changes only the spectrum. The rotation therefore arises
because each global singular vector spans several blocks,
whose singular values are rescaled differently.
Training only the block singular vectors, by contrast, is less
effective at rotating the frames. Rotating Rk leaves U and
Σ unchanged and rotates only V, whereas RLVR rotates
both frames (Zhu et al., 2026a). Rotating Lk within its
column span limits how far U can move. Dropping the
orthogonality of Rk rotates U as well but turns each Rk
into a dense factor with b times as many values as sk, losing
the parameter efficiency that on-device training requires.
Spectrum preservation. To first order, the relative change
of each singular value is a weighted average of the relative
changes of the block singular values (Proposition 1):

4.4 Spectral inheritance of the trained weights
Individual perturbations follow spectral inheritance (Sec-
tion 4.3), but the accumulated update, a fitness-weighted
sum of many such perturbations, need not preserve this struc-
ture. We verify that it does by decomposing the weights that
FuRA-S trains on MATH (Qwen2.5-Math-7B) and Count-
down (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) into rotated frames and a
changed spectrum. The accuracy gain comes almost en-
tirely from the rotated frames: combined with the pretrained
spectrum, they retain 95.1% of it on MATH and 95.8% on
Countdown (Figure 3c). Conversely, the trained spectrum
with the pretrained frames retains only 15% of the gain
on MATH and none on Countdown. The trained weights
thus exhibit spectral inheritance, as RLVR-trained models
do (Zhu et al., 2026a).
Takeaway 2: Accumulated over training, the updates
of FuRA-S still follow spectral inheritance: the rotated
frames alone retain over 95% of the accuracy gain.

Countdown. Following ES at Scale (Qiu et al., 2026),

with full-parameter ES, matching or exceeding it on the

all BP-based baselines on four of the five models, with the

MATH. We further train Qwen2.5-Math-7B under the

5.2 RLVR on quantized models
Since phones run quantized models, we adapt the re-
leased GPTQ-INT4 (Frantar et al., 2023) checkpoints of
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on Count-

compare FuRA-S with QES (Xu et al., 2026), which applies

(Figure 9 in Appendix C.3). Both ES methods also far out-

Because a specialist leaves the INT4 weights intact, serving
it adds only about 9% memory and 11–15% decoding time

5.3 Supervised and long-horizon objectives
We also examine whether the search space supports su-
pervised fine-tuning and RLVR over many turns. Super-
vised fine-tuning maximizes the likelihood of the data, as
MobileSpec does with the user’s records, but it changes
the spectrum much more than RLVR does (Figure 8c,d in
Appendix B). On Commonsense-170K (Hu et al., 2023)
with Llama-3-8B, FuRA-S outperforms all other parameter-
efficient forward-only methods under the same budget of
forward passes and exceeds the strongest of them, VeRA,
by about three points (Appendix E). The search space thus
remains effective for an objective whose weight changes do
not follow spectral inheritance. The agentic Sudoku bench-
mark of Agentic ESOpt (Zheng et al., 2026) tests RLVR over
many turns, since the agent acts for up to 45 turns and re-
ceives a single reward at the end of the episode, as in phone-
use tasks. On this benchmark, FuRA-S matches the held-out
success that Agentic ESOpt reports for full-parameter ES
with a constant perturbation scale (Appendix F).
5.4 Ablations
We ablate the block partition and the perturbation distri-
bution on Countdown with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, where
FuRA-S reaches 49.25% accuracy after 200 iterations. Re-
placing the block SVD with the SVD of the whole matrix
reduces FuRA-S to ES over the global singular values, the
search space of SVF. Its perturbations change only the spec-
trum and rotate neither frame (Section 4.3), and accuracy
drops by about four points to 45.40%. Frame rotation, which
only the block structure provides, therefore contributes to
the gain of FuRA-S. Replacing the Gaussian perturbations
with Rademacher ones, which change every block singular
value by exactly ±µrel times its pretrained value, reduces
accuracy by almost ten points to 39.60%. Since Rademacher
perturbations have the zero mean and equal variance that the
first-order analysis assumes (Corollary 1 in Appendix A),
the benefit of Gaussian perturbations comes from properties
beyond these two moments.
Takeaway 3: FuRA-S matches full-parameter ES with
at most 0.05% of the parameters, outperforms ES over
LoRA adapters or quantized weights, and surpasses BP-
based policy-gradient methods on most models.

6.1 Phone-use agents
Training a phone-use agent for its user’s tasks is the main
use case of MobileSpec. We evaluate this use case on the
GUI-only tasks of the MobileWorld benchmark (Kong et al.,
2026), which involve real Android apps such as Mastodon,
an email client, and a calendar. In each task, the agent
has up to 30 steps to accomplish a natural-language goal
on a freshly reset phone, after which a rule-based checker
inspects the device and returns the reward (Appendix C.4).
MAI-UI-2B (Zhou et al., 2025a) succeeds on only 11.1%
of these tasks, and online reinforcement learning raises the
success rate to 17.1%, as reported by Tang et al. (2026). A
user, however, needs the agent to succeed on the tasks that
they perform, so MobileSpec trains one specialist for each
of them. We train such specialists on five tasks on which
MAI-UI-2B succeeds in fewer than a quarter of the episodes.
The tasks are to reply to a Mastodon post (MastodonReply),
set an alarm from the time of an event in an email (Check-
EventTime), text a contact for a ride (RequestCarpooling),
follow a given Mastodon account (MastodonFollow), and
post a multiple-choice poll with given hashtags (Mastodon-
PostPoll). Each specialist changes only the 516,096 block
singular values of the model, 0.024% of its parameters, and
trains for 15 iterations, or 960 episodes. We compare the
step-wise fitness with episode rewards (Section 3.4).
The step-wise fitness turns tasks that the generalist rarely
solves into tasks that its specialists solve reliably (Table 3).
The specialists succeed in nearly every episode on three
tasks and improve substantially on the other two. Episode
rewards also improve on the generalist but trail the step-
wise fitness on four of the five tasks and fail almost entirely
on RequestCarpooling, since an episode reward cannot tell
which action moved the member toward the goal.

6.3 Training on an iPhone
We run MobileSpec on an iPhone 16 Pro with 8 GB of mem-
ory and train a specialist for one LaMP-2M user on top of the
released GPTQ-INT4 (Frantar et al., 2023) Llama-3.1-8B-
Instruct, following the time-based protocol of Section 6.2.
The phone reproduces the forward computation of a GPU
server, with a validation loss of 0.8505 for the generalist on
the phone and 0.8524 on the server. After 20 iterations over
10.4 hours, the validation loss drops from 0.85 to 0.19 (Fig-
ure 7a), and the specialist answers 8 of 15 held-out queries
correctly, compared with 5 for the generalist. The INT4
model occupies 5.5 GB, more than the 5.3 GB available to
an app, but since MobileSpec streams the decoder layers
from flash, training uses only 2.1 GB of resident memory
and peaks at 3.5 GB (Figure 7b). Forward computation ac-
counts for 97% of the training time, and streaming takes
only 14 seconds per iteration. Training a specialist for an 8B
model thus finishes overnight within the memory available
to an app.
Takeaway 4: MobileSpec trains specialists that reliably
solve phone-use tasks on which the generalist rarely
succeeds, and it trains per-user specialists of a few
megabytes within the memory of a phone.
7 CONCLUSION
We have presented MobileSpec, an on-device system that
trains specialist LLMs with forward passes over the INT4
model that the phone already runs. At its core, FuRA-
S trains only the block-spectral coordinates of pretrained
weights, whose independent rescaling rotates the singular
vectors of each weight matrix while largely preserving its
singular values, as RLVR does. With specialists of 1–5 MB,

Training Specialist LLMs on Your Phone: Forward-Only Optimization via Block-Spectral Coordinates
MobileSpec raises the success rate of a phone-use agent
from 6–23% up to 89–100% and trains a personalized 8B
model on an iPhone overnight. Training with forward passes
only remains slow, since each iteration evaluates a popula-
tion of perturbed models and a multi-step task multiplies
this cost by the episodes of every member and the steps
of every episode. Each task also needs a fitness that the
device can compute without labels, specialists do not yet
share what they learn, and our evaluation covers five Mo-
bileWorld tasks and six LaMP tasks. More broadly, training
specialist LLMs on personal devices becomes practical once
the search space is small enough to store for every task and
aligned with how RLVR changes pretrained weights, so that
a phone can adapt a model to its user while the user’s data
never leaves the device.

C.1 RLVR on Countdown
Experimental protocol. We follow the Countdown benchmark of ES at Scale (Qiu et al., 2026), which contains 2,200
arithmetic reasoning problems. The first 200 problems are used for training and the remaining 2,000 for evaluation. At each
iteration, all population members are evaluated on the same 200 training problems.
The prompt follows the plain-text format of ES at Scale without a chat template, requesting reasoning within <think> tags
and a final expression within <answer> tags. The reward combines answer correctness and format compliance:
r = ranswer + 0.1rformat. (13)
The answer reward equals one when the final expression uses exactly the given numbers and evaluates to the target, and zero
otherwise. The format reward equals one for the required reasoning-and-answer structure. Otherwise, a reasoning block
receives 0.1 and an answer block receives 0.5.
Training and evaluation use greedy decoding with at most 1,024 generated tokens. Each population member receives the
mean reward across the 200 training problems. Test accuracy is the fraction of problems with a correct final answer.
FuRA-S and full-parameter ES. FuRA-S trains 245,760 block singular values on Qwen2.5-0.5B, 327,680 on Llama-3.2-
1B, 508,928 on Qwen2.5-1.5B, 838,656 on Qwen2.5-3B, and 1,132,544 on Qwen2.5-7B. All five models use 15 antithetic

apply them unchanged to the other models.
The frozen spectral factors are stored in FP32, and the reconstructed weights are evaluated in BF16. For full-parameter ES,

LoRA-ES and VeRA-ES comparison. To compare the optimization spaces under the same ES procedure, we additionally

C.4 Mobile GUI Agents
Environment and model. The task runs in a MobileWorld Android emulator in Docker (Kong et al., 2026), reset before
every episode. The agent is MAI-UI-2B with its released system prompt, which asks for a thought in <thinking> tags
and one mobile use tool call per step, with click, long press, type, swipe, open, drag, system button, wait, terminate,
answer, and ask-user actions. Each step sees the task, the action history, and the recent screenshots. An episode ends on
terminate or answer, or after 30 steps, and the task’s checker scores the final device state with a partial credit between
0 and 1. An episode succeeds when its credit is 1. Generation runs in vLLM.
Step-wise fitness. After each episode, every step is reduced to a screenshot hash and an action bucket. The hash is the
256-bit difference hash of the screenshot the agent acted on (grayscale, resized to 17 × 16, signs of the horizontal gradient).
The bucket is the action type with the 108-pixel grid cell for click-like actions, the direction for swipes, and the lower-cased
argument for text, open-app, and key actions. Anchor groups are formed greedily over the pooled steps of the iteration: a
step joins the first group whose representative lies within a Hamming distance of 10, and steps in a group visited by fewer
than two distinct episodes receive zero advantage. We use γ = 0.95. An iteration pools about 1,260 steps into 136 groups
on average, 76 of them shared by at least two episodes, and 52% of steps receive a nonzero advantage.
Optimization. FuRA-S trains the block singular values of every linear projection of the language model, 516,096 values,
with 8 antithetic pairs (N = 16), J = 4 greedy episodes per member, fitness standardized over the population, and
α = 1, for 15 iterations. Greedy members still produce different episodes, because the emulator’s loading times split their
trajectories: in the first iteration, members succeed on 0 to 3 of their 4 episodes. Two vLLM servers share one GPU and
drive 12 emulators. Malformed tool calls stay at or below 1.3% of steps. Each task trains on its own GPU with the same
settings, and an iteration takes 15 to 51 minutes, almost all of it rollouts. Episode-reward ES uses the same settings, with

members sampled at temperature 0.7 under common random numbers per episode index, and scores each member by its
mean partial credit over its 4 episodes.
Evaluation. Each trained model is rebuilt from its perturbation seeds and update coefficients and runs 64 new episodes.
Base and trained models are evaluated back to back on the same 12 emulators. The trained model completes MastodonReply
in 11.5 steps on average. During training, the current model is also evaluated every 5 iterations on 16 episodes.
C.5 Personalization on LaMP
Experimental protocol. We evaluate personalization on six LaMP tasks (Salemi et al., 2024) under two splits. The
user-based split (U) follows the official LaMP partition. FuRA-S trains on the training users, selects its checkpoint on
the development users, and is tested on 2,500, 870, 2,500, 2,376, 2,500, and 1,496 queries for LaMP-1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7,
respectively. Prompts follow the LaMP templates and include the top BM25 item from the user’s own profile within a budget
of 512 tokens, whereas the model without the profile receives the input alone.
The time-based split (T) follows the release of OPPU (Tan et al., 2024). For each task, we take the 100 users with the longest
histories (101 for LaMP-3 and LaMP-5), which yields 123, 3,302, 112, 6,725, 107, and 109 test queries for LaMP-1, 2M, 3,
4, 5, and 7, respectively. Prompts follow the OPPU templates, with each field truncated to 768 words. Both splits apply the
chat template of each model, with thinking disabled for Qwen3.5-4B, and limit sequences to 2,048 tokens.
All methods sample one answer per query with temperature 0.1, top-k 10, and top-p 0.9, generating at most 200 new tokens.
We report accuracy and macro-F1 for LaMP-1, 2, and 2M, mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE)
for LaMP-3, and ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L for LaMP-4, 5, and 7.
Optimization settings. FuRA-S uses 15 antithetic pairs and µrel = 3 × 10−2 in both splits, with the mean log-likelihood
of the target tokens as fitness. In the user-based split, each task trains one model for 200 iterations with α = 1, and each
iteration evaluates 128 random training examples. The unperturbed model is scored on 256 fixed development examples at
initialization and every 20 iterations, and the best state is retained.
In the time-based split, each user’s checkpoint starts from the base model and runs for at most 20 iterations with α = 3, each
on up to 64 examples from the user’s history. The latest 10% of the history, with at least 8 records and at most half of the
history, serves as validation. Training stops after three iterations without improvement, and the best state answers the test
queries. The frozen Lk factors are stored in BF16, while sk and Rk are maintained in FP32.
Per-user LoRA baseline. We reimplement the per-user stage of OPPU. Each user receives a new rank-8 LoRA adapter on
the query and value projections, trained from the base model for two epochs over the user’s history. Training uses AdamW
with a learning rate of 10−4, weight decay of 0.01, batch size 16, LoRA scale 8, dropout 0.05, 10% warm-up followed by
linear decay, and gradient clipping at 0.3.
Computational cost. Each setting runs once with seed 42 on nodes with four NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs. On Llama-3.1-
8B-Instruct, a user-based model takes approximately 0.9 node-hours per task and up to 2.0 node-hours for LaMP-3.

Experimental protocol. We follow the multi-turn Sudoku benchmark of Agentic ESOpt (Zheng et al., 2026), using its

training puzzles and 32 held-out puzzles.
We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B in BF16 with thinking disabled. The model has 32 decoder layers comprising attention and

across the population. Generation uses temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, top-k 20, and presence penalty 1.5. Held-out performance
is evaluated every 10 iterations with three repeated evaluations.

4.2B model parameters using 32 one-sided Gaussian perturbations per iteration, with σ = α = 5 × 10−4. Both settings use
the same benchmark and number of population evaluations. Agentic ESOpt additionally decays the perturbation scale with a
cosine schedule. FuRA-S and the Vanilla ES baseline both use a constant scale, so the comparison isolates the effect of the
search space.
