Wanda-Guided Sparse Perturbations for
Efficient Zeroth-Order Optimization
Ziqing Wen1,∗, Xiaohui Song2, Jiahuan Wang1, Ping Luo1, Junlin Zeng1,
Dongsheng Li1, Tao Sun1,†
Abstract
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization enables memory-efficient fine-tuning of large language models through
forward-only evaluations, but high-dimensional random perturbations can limit optimization efficiency.
Sparse perturbations reduce the search dimension, making the selection of informative coordinates critical.
We propose Wanda-Importance Sparsity-MeZO (WIS-MeZO), which uses Wanda weight–activation
saliency to construct task-conditioned sparse perturbation masks while keeping the pretrained model
dense. We analyze support quality through gradient energy capture and relate it to gradient estimation and
convergence. Across 16 benchmarks with LLaMA and Qwen models ranging from 3B to 32B parameters,
WIS-MeZO achieves higher aggregate scores than MeZO and reaches comparable validation losses in
fewer optimization steps. Comparisons with random and magnitude-based sparse perturbations further
highlight the importance of informative support selection.
1 Introduction
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) requires substantial memory for the activations, gradients, and
optimizer states used in backpropagation. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning [Hu et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2024],
quantization [Dettmers et al., 2022], and memory-efficient optimizers [Zhao et al., 2024, Zhu et al., 2025]
reduce different parts of this cost. Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization offers another approach by estimating
update directions through forward evaluations, as demonstrated by MeZO [Malladi et al., 2023]. Its reduced
training-memory requirements are particularly appealing for on-device adaptation, where models must be
personalized under limited device resources [Guo et al., 2025].
Despite this memory advantage, ZO optimization remains challenging in the high-dimensional parameter
spaces of LLMs. The variance of random-direction gradient estimates grows with the number of perturbed
coordinates, making it difficult to obtain informative updates. Sparse ZO methods address this issue by
restricting perturbations to a subset of parameters. However, reducing the number of coordinates is only
part of the problem: the selected support must also retain useful information for downstream optimization.
Existing methods select small-magnitude weights [Liu et al., 2025] or transfer sensitivity information from
pre-training [Guo et al., 2025]. This motivates selecting sparse supports using information available from the
downstream task itself.
We propose Wanda-Importance Sparsity-MeZO (WIS-MeZO), which uses Wanda weight–activation
saliency [Sun et al., 2024] to select task-conditioned perturbation coordinates. A lightweight forward
calibration produces a row-wise sparse mask that is reused during MeZO updates, while the pretrained model
remains dense. In this way, downstream activation information guides the allocation of a fixed perturbation
budget.
To understand the effect of coordinate selection, we study gradient energy capture, which measures the
proportion of gradient energy contained in the selected support. This perspective connects support quality to
the alignment of ZO estimates and to convergence. We complement the analysis with comparisons against
alternative sparse supports and evaluations across downstream tasks and model scales.

2 Related Work
Zeroth-order optimization estimates update directions from function evaluations instead of backpropaga-
tion [Spall, 2002, Ilyas et al., 2018]. Classical approaches include ZO-SGD [Ghadimi and Lan, 2013] and
ZO-Adam [Chen et al., 2019], while MeZO [Malladi et al., 2023] enables memory-efficient LLM fine-tuning
through seed-replayed perturbations. Subsequent work improves ZO estimation through variance reduc-
tion [Gautam et al., 2024], preconditioning [Zhao et al., 2025], additional queries [Dang et al., 2026], and
low-rank or structured perturbations [Zhang et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2025]. AGZO [Lin
et al., 2026] also uses downstream activations, constructing low-rank perturbation subspaces from current
mini-batches. A complementary approach is to reduce the search dimension by selecting a sparse set of weight
coordinates.
Sparse coordinate selection has been studied in ZO optimization [Ohta et al., 2020, Gu et al., 2021, Cai
et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2024]. DeepZero [Chen et al., 2023] exploits finite-difference information, Sparse-
MeZO [Liu et al., 2025] selects small-magnitude weights, and SensZOQ [Guo et al., 2025] uses sensitivity
information from pre-training. Separately, Wanda [Sun et al., 2024] and SparseGPT [Frantar and Alistarh,
2023] study weight importance for pruning, while dynamic sparse training [Evci et al., 2020, Joo et al., 2026]
adapts sparse networks during optimization. Building on Wanda’s weight–activation score, WIS-MeZO uses
downstream data to select perturbation coordinates while keeping the model dense. We analyze the quality
of this support through gradient energy capture.

4 Methodology
Building on these two ingredients, WIS-MeZO uses Wanda scores to choose perturbation coordinates before
applying MeZO updates on the selected support. We describe the support construction and optimization
procedure below.
4.1 WIS-Guided Sparse Perturbation
For each layer W ∈ Rm×n, let M ∈ {0, 1}m×n be a binary mask. Given a density r = k/n, WIS-MeZO
selects the k largest scores in each row of S. The mask records which coordinates receive perturbations and
updates; the underlying weight matrix remains dense. Row-wise selection allocates the same perturbation
budget to every output unit.
The perturbation direction is U = (Z ⊙ M)/√r, with independent standard Gaussian entries in Z. For
one layer, this gives E∥U∥2 F = mn while restricting the support to mk coordinates. Across the model, the
corresponding support dimension is deff .

4.2 Calibration from Downstream Data
WIS-MeZO constructs the mask from 128 examples of the downstream training data using Wanda scores.
This calibration is performed once before optimization; WISD-MeZO periodically refreshes the mask every τ
steps. Appendix 7.3 examines the effect and cost of calibration size.

With in-place perturbations and seed replay, the main additional storage is the binary mask. A bit-packed
representation reduces its cost from 1 byte to 1 bit per candidate parameter, requiring less than 4GB for the
32B model. Together with one-time calibration, this compact storage makes WIS-MeZO a promising approach
for memory-constrained on-device adaptation. Section 7.1 reports peak GPU memory and throughput.
5 Theoretical Analysis
Sparse perturbations reduce the search dimension, but different supports retain different amounts of gradient
information. We analyze this trade-off through gradient energy capture, first relating Wanda scores to
gradient energy and then studying estimator alignment and convergence.
5.1 Energy Analysis
For the analysis, let Gi,j = δixj be the per-example gradient, with δi denoting the backpropagated signal and
xj an input activation. Write vj = E[x2 j ] and ˆvj for the population and empirical activation second moments.
Assumption 5.1 (Bounded Input–Error Dependence). There exist constants 0 < γ ≤ ν < ∞ such that
γE[δ2 i ]vj ≤ E[G2 i,j ] ≤ νE[δ2 i ]vj .
The constants γ and ν describe the dependence between squared inputs and squared error signals.
Independence gives γ = ν = 1.

6 Experiments
We now examine how task-conditioned support selection affects downstream fine-tuning. We evaluate five
GLUE tasks [Wang et al., 2018], SQuAD, and five SuperGLUE tasks [Wang et al., 2019], followed by
commonsense and question-answering benchmarks across larger model scales. Additional efficiency and
sensitivity results appear in the appendix.
6.1 Fine-Tuning on GLUE, SQuAD, and SuperGLUE
We evaluate WIS-MeZO and its dynamic variant WISD-MeZO on LLaMA-3.2-3B [Grattafiori et al., 2024]
and Qwen2.5-3B [Yang et al., 2024]. All ZO methods use a batch size of 16 and train for 20,000 steps.
Hyperparameters are reported in Appendix B.
The first-order baselines are full-parameter fine-tuning (FT) with AdamW [Kingma and Ba, 2015,
Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019] and LoRA [Hu et al., 2022]. The ZO baselines are MeZO [Malladi et al., 2023],
Sparse-MeZO [Liu et al., 2025], which selects small-magnitude weights, and Random-MeZO, which uses
random sparse supports. These comparisons evaluate the effect of coordinate selection within sparse ZO
fine-tuning.
Tables 1 and 2 report the task results. Figure 1 compares validation loss against optimization steps over
five random seeds.
WIS-MeZO improves the average score over MeZO, Random-MeZO, and Sparse-MeZO in all four model–
benchmark combinations. On LLaMA-3.2-3B, its average scores are 79.1 on the GLUE/SQuAD tasks and
77.3 on SuperGLUE, compared with 77.7 and 74.5 for Sparse-MeZO. The corresponding Qwen2.5-3B scores
are 86.9 and 81.7, compared with 86.6 and 80.3. The size of the gain varies across backbones and tasks, with
the larger improvements occurring on LLaMA-3.2-3B.
Beyond final task scores, the validation-loss curves in Figure 1 show that WIS-MeZO reaches comparable
loss levels in fewer optimization steps than MeZO on RTE, MNLI, and BoolQ. This improvement is consistent
with the gradient-energy results in Section 5. Runtime and memory measurements are reported in Section 7.1.
Periodic recalibration in WISD-MeZO increases the average score in all four model–benchmark combina-
tions. Fixed-mask WIS-MeZO is the primary method, and WISD-MeZO characterizes the effect of refreshing
the support during training.

8 Conclusion
We present WIS-MeZO, which uses downstream Wanda scores to select sparse perturbation coordinates
for memory-efficient LLM fine-tuning. Our gradient-energy analysis explains the importance of support
selection, while experiments across 16 benchmarks and models from 3B to 32B show improved aggregate
performance over MeZO. By combining forward-only updates with one-time calibration and compact mask
storage, WIS-MeZO also offers a promising direction for resource-constrained on-device adaptation.

B Experimental Details
For the GLUE, SQuAD, and SuperGLUE experiments, all ZO methods train for 20,000 steps with a batch
size of 16; FT and LoRA use a batch size of 8. Checkpoints are selected by validation performance. We use
a sequence length of 2048, constant learning rates, and zero weight decay for ZO methods, following the
settings in MeZO and Sparse-MeZO [Malladi et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2025].
Tables 11–13 report the hyperparameter search and selected configurations. Results on GLUE and SQuAD
are averaged over five seeds, while SuperGLUE results are from one run. WIS-MeZO uses 128 calibration
examples from downstream training data and a fixed mask.
Regarding the computing environment, we use Python 3.10.14 and PyTorch 2.8.0 [Paszke et al., 2019].
All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA H20 GPUs.
C Additional Gradient Energy Capture Results
We compare Random, W-small, W-large, and WIS supports at matched density. The supports are selected
uniformly at random, by small weight magnitude, by large weight magnitude, and by the Wanda score,
respectively. For each task, we evaluate their gradient energy capture on 2,000 gradient snapshots from an
auxiliary AdamW training run on LLaMA-3.2-3B. The supports are fixed at initialization, and all strategies
are evaluated on the same gradients.
For a support mask M and a mini-batch gradient Gt, energy capture is measured by
ρt(M) = ∥M ⊙ Gt∥2 F ∥Gt∥2 F . (11)
We report the average over optimization steps. Random masking captures an expected fraction r of gradient
energy. At r = 0.05, WIS preserves over 40% of the energy and exceeds the other selection rules. W-small
also outperforms W-large and random masking.
Figures 3–5 report the additional results at density ratios of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4, respectively.
WIS captures the most gradient energy in nearly all reported task–density settings. Its advantage is largest
at low densities. At r = 0.4 on MNLI, WIS and W-small have comparable capture ratios. W-small consistently
captures more energy than W-large and generally exceeds random masking. These comparisons describe
how the selected coordinates align with the shared AdamW gradient snapshots and show the contribution of
downstream activation information to support selection.

Algorithm 2 Perturb Parameters
1: Function PerturbParameters(W, r, ϵ, M, srng):
2: Input: Weights W ∈ Rm×n, density ratio r, perturbation scale ϵ, mask M ∈ {0, 1}m×n, and random
seed srng
3: Reset the random number generator with seed srng
4: Sample Z ∈ Rm×n with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries
5: W ← W + ϵ√r Z ⊙ M {In-place perturbation}
6: return W

5: k ← ⌊nr⌋ {Selected parameters per row}
