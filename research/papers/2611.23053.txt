SPICE: SPARSE PARAMETER GOSSIP FOR LLM PRETRAINING
Pietro Cagnasso 1 2 Daniele Kopyshevskiy 3 Edouard Oyallon 3 4 Eugene Belilovsky 1 2
ABSTRACT
Pretraining language models across geographically distributed resources requires communication-efficient methods
that operate under limited and variable internet bandwidth. Decentralized training replaces global collectives with
peer exchanges, but full-model messages make larger peer neighborhoods costly. We introduce SPICE, which
combines sparse parameter gossip with error feedback through corrected parameter values that remain directly
interpretable as peer neighborhoods change. These sparse exchanges make broader neighborhoods affordable,
achieving better training quality with less communication than the evaluated dense decentralized baselines. As the
replica count grows, SPICE increases the number of peers contacted while keeping individual messages sparse,
limiting the quality degradation observed with dense gossip. Under bandwidth constraints, SPICE achieves higher
training throughput than DDP and dense gossip, while sustaining throughput during transient link slowdowns.
1 INTRODUCTION
Training large language models at scale relies on distributing
computation across many model replicas, each potentially
spanning several GPUs. In standard synchronous data par-
allel training, these replicas average their gradients before
each optimizer step (Li et al., 2020), typically through an
All-Reduce collective (Patarasuk & Yuan, 2009). Each step
therefore requires communication among all replicas and
waits for the slowest participant, making training efficiency
dependent on the connectivity between them.
Even within tightly connected GPU clusters, communi-
cation bandwidth can limit training efficiency, motivat-
ing low-precision transfers to reduce communication vol-
ume (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). These constraints become
more severe when training extends over geographically dis-
tributed data centers, where bandwidth can be limited and
vary across participants. Recent large language model pre-
training runs over the internet (Jaghouar et al., 2024; Lidin
et al., 2026) demonstrate the opportunity to pool resources
across sites, but exploiting these resources efficiently re-
quires limiting the cost of communication (Nabli et al., 2024;
Kopyshevskiy et al., 2026).
Decentralized (gossip) training replaces the typical global
All-Reduce operation with exchanges among selected
peers (Koloskova et al., 2020). This has several potential
advantages as it can avoid communication bottlenecks, sup-
port heterogeneous bandwidth, and maintain robustness to
failure of individual links. However, combining gossip meth-
ods with adaptive optimizers has historically been difficult,

pretraining foundational models. Recent works by Wang
et al. (2025) and Cagnasso et al. (2026) have shown that

empirically shows that communicating with more peers can
improve training quality (Cagnasso et al., 2026). With full
model exchanges, however, each additional peer increases
communication volume, motivating smaller messages that
allow broader peer coverage at a lower communication cost
(Figure 2).
One approach to reducing communication is compression,
through sparsification or quantization. Sparsification trans-
mits only a subset of gradient or pseudogradient coordi-
nates, selected, for example, through chunked TOP-k selec-
tion (Sarfi et al., 2026). The resulting information loss can
be mitigated through error feedback, which retains omitted
update components for later communication (Stich & Karim-
ireddy, 2020). Applying these mechanisms to parameter
gossip is challenging because replicas evolve from different
models: a compressed update relative to the sender’s model
cannot generally be applied directly to the receiver’s model.
Methods such as CHOCO-SGD address this mismatch by
reconstructing communicated states from accumulated com-
pressed updates on a fixed communication graph (Koloskova
et al., 2019), but changing peers requires making the relevant
reconstruction state available to new receivers. After a local
update, sparsification lets a sender transmit selected param-
eter values directly, reducing message size while allowing
any receiver to interpret them without a shared reference

model. This motivates sparse parameter gossip that com-

The framework of Cagnasso et al. (2026) provides a nat-
ural way to realize this design: it expresses gossip as a
local pseudo-gradient formed from differences between a
node’s parameters and those received from its peers. This
formulation allows us to apply a sparse update with error
feedback locally, then communicate the resulting parameter
values at the selected coordinates. We use this mechanism
to introduce SPICE, a decentralized training algorithm that
combines chunked TOP-k sparsification with error feedback
while retaining the Nesterov outer optimizer for consensus
acceleration. The transmitted parameter values incorpo-
rate accumulated error corrections and can be interpreted
directly by any receiving peer, without persistent reconstruc-
tion caches.
Our contributions are:
1. We propose SPICE, a decentralized training algorithm
that integrates sparsification and error memory into
parameter gossip through corrected absolute model
endpoints.
2. We empirically show that SPICE achieves lower valida-
tion loss than evaluated decentralized baselines, while
transmitting less data (Figure 1).
3. We show that SPICE reduces end-to-end training time
to a fixed token budget across several bandwidth caps
compared with dense GASLoC and DDP, and retains
most of the training throughput under transient band-
width reductions, including severe slowdowns.

Compressed gossip. WASH communicates selected
weights among models through random peer-to-peer permu-
tations, keeping the models compatible with final weight
averaging (Fournier et al., 2024). Compressed gossip meth-
ods instead combine neighboring models during training,
with CHOCO-SGD reconstructing model estimates by ac-
cumulating compressed differences (Koloskova et al., 2019;
Koloskova* et al., 2020). Each message therefore depends
on the receiver’s reconstruction history, so the standard for-
mulation assumes a fixed communication graph to ensure
that receivers retain the history needed. SPICE instead aver-
ages selected absolute endpoint values that incorporate error
memory and require no receiver reconstruction history.
Communication topology. Beyond message compres-
sion, topology design controls the communication cost of
propagating information among replicas. MATCHA sam-
ples graph matchings to balance communication cost and
convergence (Wang et al., 2019), while one-peer exponen-
tial graphs and EquiTopo provide sparse communication
schemes with favorable mixing properties (Ying et al., 2021;
Song et al., 2022). However, faster consensus alone does
not establish better training quality, as the spectral gap can
fail to predict decentralized learning performance under
identically distributed data (Vogels et al., 2023).

Randomized Coordinate-Wise Gossip and Outer Opti-
mization. In methods such as CHOCO-SGD, messages
depend on accumulated reconstruction state, which must
be available when introducing a new peer. The corrected
absolute endpoints in SPICE can instead be interpreted by
any receiver without previous exchanges. This allows peer
neighborhoods to change between rounds. We use random-
ized peer selection, motivated by analyses showing that ran-
domized one- and two-peer exchanges can mix efficiently
as the number of replicas grows (Cagnasso et al., 2026).
At each round, a newly sampled shared permutation deter-
mines the neighborhoods, and each replica sends its selected
endpoint values to q peers.
Each message contains a fraction δ of the model coordi-
nates, giving qδd transmitted parameter values per round
(Figure 2). CHOCO-SGD can match this payload using a
fixed graph with the same peer count and density. How-
ever, sparse fixed topologies such as rings can limit mixing
and hinder training as the number of replicas grows (Nabli
et al., 2023). The advantage of SPICE is that it combines
sparse messages with changing communication partners
without transferring reconstruction state. Relative to the
complete-graph CHOCO-SGD baseline used in our experi-
ments, contacting only q peers reduces outbound volume by
a factor of (R − 1)/q at equal message density.

3.2 Communication Cost
Each worker saves its round-start parameters to compute
the displacement used for support selection and the pseudo-
gradient used by the outer optimizer. For selection, the
trainable local parameter shards are viewed as one vec-
tor and partitioned into chunks of b coordinates. Chunks
can cross PyTorch’s parameter boundaries, so participating
workers have to use a consistent parameter ordering and
shard layout.
The selected coordinates become a message containing two
buffers: their indices and their parameter values. For b ≤
4096, an index needs only 12 bits to identify a position
within its chunk; thus, two indices can be packed into three
bytes. Since each chunk contributes the TOP-k entries in
order, the receiver infers the chunk from the entry’s position
in the message.

4.2 Training Results
For the 134M model, SPICE achieves the best validation
loss among both the sparse and decentralized methods eval-
uated in Table 2a. Increasing the replica count from 8 to
128 raises validation loss by only 1.3% for SPICE, com-
pared with 5.6% for dense two-peer GASLoC. This reduces
a known difficulty of methods using inner and outer opti-
mizers, whose performance can degrade as the replica count
increases (Horoi et al., 2026; Belilovsky et al., 2026). Each
message contains only 3.125% of the parameter coordinates,
transmitting more than 30 times fewer parameter values than
a dense decentralized exchange. This sparsity allows us to
expand the neighborhood proportionally to the replica count,
using q = R/2, at a fraction of the volume required for
dense exchanges with the same peers. As the fixed global
batch is divided among more replicas, the neighborhood
grows by the same factor that the local batch size decreases,
keeping the total number of tokens processed by the commu-
nicating peers constant. For each coordinate, however, only
peers that select it contribute to the outer gradient, so this
does not imply constant token coverage at every coordinate.
The token coverage maintained across the neighborhood
provides a possible explanation for the reduced degradation,
which results in a validation loss advantage of 0.14 over
dense two-peer GASLoC at 128 replicas.
Using the same density and neighborhood rule, SPICE re-
tains the best validation loss among the evaluated sparse
methods when the model size increases from 134M to 551M
parameters, as shown in Table 2b. As with the 134M model,
its performance is comparable to dense two-peer GASLoC
at smaller replica counts, with an increasing advantage as
the replica count grows. From 8 to 32 replicas, its validation
loss increases by 1.9%, compared with 3.4% for the dense
variant, extending the reduced degradation to a larger model
and training budget.
Table 3 extends the evaluation to OLMoE-1B-7B (Muen-
nighoff et al., 2025), a mixture-of-experts model with 7B
parameters. With 8 replicas, SPICE exchanges 6.25% of
the parameter coordinates with four peers per round, using
one-eighth the communication volume than dense two-peer
GASLoC. It achieves a nearly identical validation loss and
comparable accuracy in HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019) and
PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019), with both accuracy differences
falling within the reported error bars. These results show
that the communication savings also extend to different
and larger model architectures while preserving competitive
training quality.

4.4 Training Under Limited Bandwidth
Training over the internet often relies on connections
with substantially less bandwidth than datacenter intercon-
nects (Wang et al., 2023; Jaghouar et al., 2024; Lidin et al.,
2026). In this setting, communication can limit training
speed, while bandwidth stragglers and temporary link fail-
ures can further delay synchronization. These constraints
motivate measuring how reducing communication volume
affects training time.
4.4.1 End-to-End Training Time
We benchmark the 134M and 551M models on eight
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs within a single machine, with
one training replica per GPU, using our TorchTitan im-
plementation (Liang et al., 2025) with actual peer-to-peer
communication. For SPICE, each exchange transmits the
selected parameter values and their 12-bit packed indices.
The timings therefore include index packing, transmission
of both indices and values, and unpacking and accumulation
at the receiver. To control bandwidth, we place each replica
in a separate Linux network namespace and connect the
namespaces through a virtual Ethernet bridge. We use tc
netem to cap outgoing traffic at the replica interface and
incoming traffic at the corresponding interface on the host.
The specified rate therefore limits each replica’s aggregate
sending and receiving bandwidth separately, and is shared
by all its peer exchanges. We apply the same rate to all eight
replicas and evaluate caps of 1, 2, 5, and 10 Gbps. NCCL
uses its socket transport over these interfaces, with direct
GPU transfers, shared memory transport, and InfiniBand
disabled to prevent communication from bypassing the caps.
These runs impose bandwidth limits without injecting addi-
tional latency or link failures. The uncapped setting instead
uses NCCL without the namespace bandwidth controls.
Figure 4 shows that SPICE achieves the highest training
throughput for both model sizes at every tested bandwidth.
Its throughput changes little as bandwidth decreases from
10 to 1 Gbps, while all dense methods slow down over

bandwidth reduction on the paths connecting two islands,

other islands remain available. When several exchanges
share the affected network infrastructure, the slowdown can

Improving throughput with bounded waiting. The re-
sults above wait for all requested peer contributions, but
peer-to-peer exchanges allow each replica to aggregate avail-
able contributions without waiting for every requested peer.
To exploit this flexibility under communication stragglers,
one could use a windowed approach to gossip, which com-
bines background reception with a bounded communication
window. Each replica posts a receive operation in the back-
ground so that peers can send updates before it reaches its
own exchange stage. At the deadline, it aggregates only
complete peer contributions and proceeds with its outer up-
date, using only its own endpoint if none arrived in time, as
it would do for coordinates that were not shared by any other
peer. A global collective instead requires the prescribed par-
ticipation to complete its result, even when communication
overlaps with computation.
Sparsification allows each replica to contact more peers
under the same communication budget, increasing the op-
portunities to receive contributions before the deadline. This
benefit depends on reaching peers through unaffected paths,
since peers sharing a bottleneck can be delayed together.
We therefore evaluate SPICE with windowed gossip in the
same island setup. During the initial healthy period, we
measure the distribution of complete sparse exchange times.
The communication window is fixed at 2× the measured
99th percentile and kept unchanged during the period of
reduced bandwidth to accommodate small differences in
replica timing. Figure 6c shows that windowed gossip fur-
ther improves throughput under the more severe slowdown
by allowing updates to proceed with fewer contributions.
With this window, committed updates used an average of
2.48 ± 0.88 peer contributions out of four requested peers,
while retaining approximately 86% of healthy throughput.
5 CONCLUSION
We introduced SPICE, a decentralized training method that
combines sparse parameter exchanges with error feedback
to make broader peer neighborhoods affordable. Under tight
communication budgets, exchanging fewer coordinates with
more peers improves training quality over denser exchanges
with fewer peers. This design can outperform dense gos-
sip while communicating less and limiting quality degrada-
tion as the replica count grows. Our implementation turns
these smaller exchanges into higher training throughput
under bandwidth constraints and maintains much of this
performance during transient link slowdowns. These re-
sults support decentralized language model pretraining over
bandwidth-constrained networks.

Tables 4 and 5 extend Table 2 with validation losses for

A.1 Expanding neighborhoods reduces degradation
with replica count
We examine how the number of contacted peers affects
training quality as the replica count increases. Tables 4
and 5 report validation losses across replica and peer counts
at a fixed message density of 3.125%. For the 134M model,
increasing the replica count from 8 to 128 while keeping
four peers raises validation loss by 10.3%. Expanding the
neighborhood according to q = R/2 instead limits this
increase to 1.3%. At fixed message density, expanding the
neighborhood therefore reduces the degradation observed
when training is distributed across more replicas.
Figure 7 groups configurations by their peer batch size,
qB/R, where B = 1024 sequences is the fixed global batch.
Along each curve, the peer count grows proportionally to the
replica count, keeping the total batch processed by the con-
tacted peers constant while each replica’s local batch shrinks.
Validation loss remains approximately stable across these
configurations, with larger peer batches consistently yield-
ing lower loss. These results are consistent with expanding
neighborhoods compensating for the shrinking local batch,
although they do not isolate the mechanism responsible. In
particular, peer batch size is not an effective optimization
batch: peers update different local models, and only those
transmitting a given coordinate contribute to its aggregation.
At peer batch sizes of 32 and 64 sequences, validation loss
decreases slightly as the neighborhood expands. This sug-
gests that peer batch size alone does not fully determine
quality. The trend is consistent with Figure 3, where allo-
cating a fixed communication volume to more peers with
sparser messages yields lower validation loss.
The 551M results in Table 5 confirm at a larger model scale
that expanding the neighborhood reduces validation-loss
degradation as the replica count increases.
B REPRODUCIBILITY
B.1 Peer sampling
The SAMPLE PEERS procedure in Algorithm 1 is imple-
mented as follows. At each round t, every replica inde-
pendently generates the same uniform permutation σ(t) of
the R replicas using a shared seed and the round index t.
This permutation arranges replicas on a cycle without com-
munication to coordinate peer selection. For an even peer
count q < R, each replica exchanges messages with the
q/2 replicas preceding it and the q/2 replicas following it,
with positions taken modulo R. For the one-peer GASLoC
configuration, R must be even, and consecutive replicas
in the permutation form disjoint pairs: the first exchanges
with the second, the third with the fourth, and so on. Both
constructions give each replica exactly q distinct peers and
make every connection symmetric. Generating a fresh per-
mutation at each round changes the communication partners
while preserving the peer count, allowing information to
propagate beyond a fixed neighborhood.
B.2 Software and hardware setup
The training simulator uses Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.10.0,
and TorchTitan 0.2.1. The implementation used for timing
measurements is based on TorchTitan 0.2.2 and the June
23, 2026 nightly release of torchft. All models use
the LLaMA-2 tokenizer with a vocabulary of 32K tokens,
available from the Together AI repository.
Training runs for the 134M and 551M models use compute
nodes with four NVIDIA H100 GPUs per node, connected
through NVLink within each node and InfiniBand across
nodes. These runs simulate multiple logical replicas on
each GPU. OLMoE training runs use a single node with
eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs connected through NVLink.
Training times and throughput are measured separately on
a single node with eight NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, with
one physical training replica per GPU.

B.3 Communication bandwidth limits
For the global bandwidth experiments, each of the eight
GPU replicas runs in a separate Linux network namespace
connected through a virtual Ethernet pair to a private bridge.
Linux traffic control tc applies the same fixed bandwidth
cap to every replica, independently limiting its aggregate
outgoing and incoming traffic. The outgoing cap is installed
on the namespace interface, and the incoming cap on the
host end of its virtual Ethernet pair, using netem with a
queue limit of 10,000 packets and packet offloads disabled.
All peer connections of a replica share these caps. NCCL
uses IPv4 sockets on the virtual interface, with any other
GPU transport technology disabled to prevent communi-
cation from bypassing the limits. Control and monitoring
traffic also share the capped interfaces.
For the selective bandwidth experiment, we compare DDP
and SPICE using the same namespace isolation and assign
replicas 1–3 to island A, 4–6 to island B, and 7–8 to island
C (Figure 6a). After five minutes of unrestricted communi-
cation, a host controller caps only traffic between islands A
and B at 500 Mbit/s (or 100 Mbit/s) for five minutes, then
removes the caps for five minutes of recovery. Each source
interface applies a separate bandwidth cap to each affected
peer based on its destination IP address. The cap applies
independently in each direction, while all other worker pairs
remain unrestricted. Transitions follow a common mono-
tonic host clock independently of training progress. DDP
uses TorchFT with NCCL sockets, while SPICE exchanges
sparse payloads with four rotating peers through a TCP
transport that stages tensors in CPU memory. A calibration
during the initial healthy phase sets the exchange deadline
of the windowed version of SPICE to twice the pooled 99th
percentile of exchange duration. Only complete payloads
with matching attempt and schema identities received before
the deadline contribute to the update, allowing communi-
cation with healthy peers to remain useful when capped
transfers miss the deadline.

B.4 Random seeds and reporting precision
We evaluate each training configuration using a single ran-
dom seed. Recent pretraining studies likewise report single-
seed results at selected model scales, citing the computa-
tional cost of repeated runs (Loeschcke et al., 2024; Glad-
stone et al., 2026). Given this cost, we prioritize allocating
our computational budget to hyperparameter tuning rather
than repeated training across seeds. Related distributed pre-
training studies also report their main comparisons without
across-seed uncertainty estimates (Douillard et al., 2024;
Kolehmainen et al., 2026; Janson et al., 2026).
As a closely related reference, GASLoC evaluates 134M-
parameter models with 16 replicas over four random
seeds, reporting validation-loss standard deviations of at
most 0.011 across the evaluated methods and configura-
tions (Cagnasso et al., 2026). Following its main-result
reporting convention, we report validation losses rounded
to two decimal places, evaluated on a held-out set of 4M
tokens for the 134M and 551M models.
B.5 Hyperparameters
We select hyperparameters by grid search using validation
loss on a second separate evaluation set of 1M tokens. For
the 134M model, we search the peak inner learning rate
over {1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5, 6} × 10−3, the outer learning rate
over {0.8, 0.9, 1.0}, and the outer Nesterov momentum over
{0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. For the 551M model, the corresponding
learning rate grids are {3, 4, 5, 6} × 10−3 and {0.8, 0.9},
with the same outer momentum grid. The inner learning
rate warms up from one thousandth of its peak over the first
10% of training steps, followed by cosine decay to 10%
of the peak. The inner optimizer is AdamW with β1 =
0.9. Following Belilovsky et al. (2026), we set β2 to 0.95,
0.975, 0.987, 0.994, and 0.997 for R = 8, 16, 32, 64, 128,
respectively. For OLMoE, after short preliminary runs to
assess initial training stability, we fix the peak inner learning
rate to 2 × 10−3, with warmup over the first 5% of training
steps and the same initial and final learning rate fractions.
We use β1 = β2 = 0.9, an outer learning rate of 0.9, and
outer Nesterov momentum of 0.2.
Across all models, AdamW uses ϵ = 10−8 and weight decay
of 0.1. Inner gradients are clipped to a norm of 1.0, while
the outer optimizer uses no gradient clipping and a constant
learning rate. Unless specified otherwise, the TOP-k block
size is fixed to b = 4096, and the error feedback decay
factor is fixed to 0.95 following Sarfi et al. (2026). Table 7
reports the selected hyperparameters.
