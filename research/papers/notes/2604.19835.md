# 2604.19835 — Expert Upcycling: Shifting the Compute-Efficient Frontier of Mixture-of-Experts (Expert Upcycling)

## 1. Citation

- **Title:** Expert Upcycling: Shifting the Compute-Efficient Frontier of Mixture-of-Experts
- **Authors:** Chaitanya Dwivedi, Binxuan Huang, Himanshu Gupta, Pratik Jayarao, Neeraj Varshney, Bing Yin
  (Amazon Stores Foundation AI; the affiliation list also gives Carnegie Mellon University and Anthropic)
- **Date:** arXiv v1 21 Apr 2026 (checked on the abs page). Notes are from v2 (10 May 2026). Preprint.
- **Code:** https://github.com/amazon-science/expert-upcycling (footnote 1). Web search shows the README states a
  CC-BY-NC-4.0 license and "27,888 vs 41,328 GPU hours". Neither appears in the paper (unverified).

## 2. Summary

The paper grows a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model mid-training by **doubling the number of experts while keeping
top-K fixed** (the number of experts each token uses). Total capacity rises; per-token compute and inference cost do
not change. The operator copies existing experts, giving extra copies to experts with the largest loss gradient. It
copies the router rows for the copies and adds tiny noise to their router biases. Optimizer state is reset, and
continued pretraining (CPT) follows. Ordinary training noise plus "loss-free" load balancing makes the copies
diverge. At 7B→13B total (32→64 experts, about 1B active, 380B pretraining tokens), the grown model matches a
64-expert model trained from scratch on validation loss (1.263 vs 1.267) and comes within 0.3 points on 11
benchmarks, using about 24–32% fewer GPU-hours. A side result: dense→MoE "sparse upcycling" at matched tokens did
worse than even the smaller MoE trained from scratch (Table 4).

## 3. Method

- **Schedule (§3, Fig. 1):** train an E-expert MoE for τ steps, apply the operator U_m (m = 2), then train the
  mE-expert model for T−τ steps. Both arms see the same tokens. Saving = τ·(s_mE − s_E), where s is the time per step
  (Eq. 1–2).
- **Operator (Def. 3.1):** (1) copy expert e r_e ≥ 1 times, with Σ r_e = mE. (2) Copy each source expert's router
  vector to its replicas and add uniform noise U(−δ, δ), δ = 1e-3, to the replicas' router biases only. (3) Leave
  attention, embeddings, norms and dense layers unchanged. The loss right after expansion is close to the loss
  before it (gap below 1e-2; §3.2).
- **Utility-based allocation (§3.3, §4):** score each expert by u_G(e) = ||∇_{w_e} L||², the squared gradient norm
  averaged over 10 batches at τ. Hand out copies greedily, at most 3 per expert. Weight × gradient saliency scores
  about the same. Curvature-normalized and weight-norm-only scores do worse (Table 3, App. B).
- **Training details (§4):** optimizer state is reset at τ. Loss-free load balancing (router-bias updates, no
  auxiliary loss) is used throughout, so every replica gets a gradient. Learning rate follows warmup–stable–decay
  (WSD), with a 10% anneal at the end of each stage. Training uses data and tensor parallelism on an A100 cluster.
- **Theory (§3.2, App. A):** assuming convexity and bounded gradients, the regret-bound gap equals (I) a capacity gap,
  weighted by the share of learning-rate mass spent in the small model, plus (II) an initialization gain that depends
  on how close the copies start to the optimum. The paper uses this to argue: grow early enough, leave enough
  CPT, and copy the right experts.
- **Main model (Table 5):** 20-layer interleaved MoE (dense and MoE layers alternate), hidden size 2048, 10 MoE
  layers, expert FFN (feed-forward) size 3072, top-2, vocabulary 200,704, context 8,192. At 32 experts it has 1.98B
  active and 7.64B total parameters including embeddings.

## 4. Key reported results

- **7B→13B (Table 1):** pretrain 380B tokens with 32 experts, then continue on a separate curated CPT split
  (instruction following, reasoning, math) for 190B tokens (50%) or 380B (100%).

  | Metric | Fixed-32 | Upcycled 32→64 | Fixed-64 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Val loss, 50% CPT | 1.339 | 1.305 | 1.308 |
  | Avg accuracy, 11 tasks, 50% CPT | 48.5 | 52.1 | 53.4 |
  | Val loss, 100% CPT | 1.301 | 1.263 | 1.267 |
  | Avg accuracy, 100% CPT | 52.9 | 56.4 | 56.7 |

  GPU-hours saved: about 32% (50% CPT) and 24% (100% CPT). If the small checkpoint already exists, savings are about
  67% at τ ≈ 2/3·T and 50% at τ = 1/2·T (§3.1, §6). Step time is about 2.2 s at 32 experts and 4.2 s at 64 (§3.1).
- **Warm start (§5.1):** right after expansion, training loss is 1.38, against 1.32 for the source and 10.5 for a
  randomly initialized 64-expert model. It drops below Fixed-32 within 22B CPT tokens (about 6% of pretraining). At
  50% CPT the remaining gap sits in MMLU, BBH, GSM8K and IFEval.
- **When to grow (Table 2a; 10-layer MoE, about 1B total / 144M active, T = 100K steps):** growing at τ/T = 0.13–0.38
  closes 100% of the gap; 0.05 closes 81% and 0.51 closes 98%.
- **How much training after growth (Table 2b):** CPT at 10%→100% of the pretraining steps raises gap closure from
  34.7% to 98.0%. The paper says at least 50% is needed for strong closure.
- **Which experts to copy (Table 3):** initializing the new experts randomly is worse than not growing (3.107 vs 2.857
  at about 25% CPT). The gradient-norm score triples gap closure over uniform copying at about 25% CPT (26.5% vs
  8.2%). At 100% CPT, gradient-norm copying ends at 2.759 vs 2.769 for uniform copying (98% vs 78% gap closure,
  our arithmetic). Twenty diversity heuristics (noise, drop-upcycling, SVD (singular value decomposition),
  orthogonalization) beat plain copying by at most 1e-3 in loss, and some are slightly worse (App. D, Table 8). Loss
  at the start and at the end correlate (Spearman ρ = 0.80).
- **MoE→MoE vs dense→MoE (Table 4; 8-layer, top-1, uniform copies):** expert upcycling ends within 0.005–0.020 of
  Fixed-mE. Sparse upcycling ends worse than Fixed-E at every activation ratio (the share of experts each token
  uses). The gap between the two methods grows from 0.026 (25% activation) to 0.241 (3.13%).
- **Full MoE, 256→512 experts, top-8 (Table 7):** gap closure of 93.8%, 95.3% and 92.9% at 154M, 305M and 1.03B
  total parameters.

## 5. Relevance to Crowd

- **A pivot operator for sparse epochs that keeps per-token work fixed.** Doubling experts at fixed top-K lets total
  parameters track network size while each node's work per token stays flat. That is one precondition for reaching a
  million GPUs with 8–24 GB cards. The paper itself suggests repeated ×2 growth (§6).
- **Fits expert ownership (SPES, 2602.11543).** When the volunteer count doubles, each newcomer can own a copy of an
  existing high-utility expert, so growth needs no new random weights. Copies start identical, so early on two owners
  of sibling experts should report similar updates, which could serve as a cheap cross-check (our idea, untested).
- **Challenges the roadmap's E3→E4 plan.** The roadmap stays dense until 9B, then upcycles once to a ~27B MoE
  (`05-scale-roadmap.md` §2). Here, at matched tokens, dense→MoE did worse than a half-size MoE trained from scratch
  (Table 4, small models). That favors **going sparse early (E1/E2) and then growing by expert duplication**. SPES's
  successful dense→9B upcycle from a heavily trained open checkpoint suggests the answer depends on the regime;
  worth a small A/B test.
- **Determinism for "pure function of the record" pivots** (roadmap §4). Copying plus seeded bias noise is
  deterministic. The utility scores need gradients on fixed batches, which may not reproduce bit-for-bit across GPU
  types. Preferred: compute the scores redundantly and record the agreed scores on the ledger, since 10 batches is
  cheap. Fallback: uniform copying, at a cost of about 0.005–0.014 in validation loss (Table 3).
- **Timing rules for pivots:** grow at τ/T ≈ 0.13–0.5 and plan at least 50% of the pre-growth tokens after growth
  (Table 2). The optimizer reset makes growth a network-wide event, so it belongs at a pivot boundary.
- **Pivot sanity gate:** loss just after growth should sit near the source loss (1.38 vs 1.32 here), far from
  random-init levels. A Gauntlet-style held-out check (Covenant-72B, 2603.08163) could refuse a malformed growth step
  (our idea).
- **vs round 1:** SOMA (2609.37899) adds capacity as independent ensemble members; here experts stay inside one
  routed model. Decoupled DiLoCo (2604.21428) trained MoEs, but growth under DiLoCo-style local steps, compression or
  zero-order (DUST) updates is untested.

## 6. Caveats

- Only m = 2 and at most 7B→13B. Training is centralized on A100s. There are no tests of low bandwidth, asynchrony,
  heterogeneous hardware, Byzantine peers, or growth while many replicas train apart.
- The 7B continued-pretraining data differs from the pretraining data (curated mix), so benchmark gains partly
  reflect that data. All three arms do see the same tokens.
- Seeds and error bars are not reported. Most ablations use about 1B-total / 144M-active models and validation loss
  only.
- The main result uses an interleaved MoE; the full-MoE check reaches only about 1B total (Table 7).
- The dense→MoE comparison (Table 4) uses tiny 8-layer top-1 models with uniform copies. Stabilized sparse-upcycling
  variants were not tested (§5.2.3), so do not extrapolate it to 9B→27B.
- The theory assumes convexity (App. A.3). The method depends on loss-free load balancing.
- The README's GPU-hour figures and license are not stated in the paper.
